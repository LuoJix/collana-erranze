## Prologue: "Cossa fat, zughet?"

In 1983 I was working on the health information system of the Province of Trento: the body of data that health services and hospitals collect about what they do and for whom. Trento is a province in the Italian Alps, between Verona and the German-speaking South Tyrol, with fewer than half a million inhabitants and an autonomy that most Italian territories do not have: it makes its own laws in many fields and runs its own health service with its own budget. I worked in its Health Department, and that year I began to work at a terminal. It was a screen with a keyboard and no computing power of its own; it served to connect to a mainframe, as we said then, a large central computer that stood somewhere else and did the sums for everyone. On the mainframe ran SAS, a suite of programs for statistical analysis, and to learn it I had taken a four-day course in Venice. Then I had come back to Trento and begun to spend many hours of the day in front of that screen.

I remember that one day a senior official, passing my office, stopped in the doorway and asked me, in Trentino dialect: "Cossa fat, zughet?" What's this, then — playing? He said it half in jest and half in earnest, with a touch of embarrassment. The embarrassment was his, not mine. He saw a civil servant bent over a lit screen with no papers on the desk, and he did not quite know what he was looking at.

What I was looking at, mostly, were the admission and discharge records of hospital patients: who went into hospital, where, for what reason, for how many days, how they came out and with what diagnosis. Until then those records had been processed by the Province's computing centre on specific request. Anyone who wanted a piece of information had to ask for it, wait, and receive exactly what had been asked for on a printout, the long sheets of continuous paper that came out of the printers of the time. Having SAS meant being able to do it oneself, with more independence and more flexibility: try a question, look at the result, ask another at once. With a few doctors interested in epidemiology I also carried out studies on specific subjects. They brought the questions, in a personal capacity, because something had caught their curiosity or worried them. There was no office that formulated those questions and no programme that put them in order. There was a person with a question, another with a terminal, and an archive.

Three years later that work acquired a title and an audience. On 18 December 1986, at a study day on new technologies and the management of local government held in the Sala Giulia in Trento, the programme gave me a talk at noon on "The hospital admission and discharge procedure in the Province of Trento". On the programme sheet I am still "dott. Gianni Martini", of the Province's Health Service. On that occasion I met Gino Colazzo, professor of computer science at the University of Trento, who spoke in the afternoon; I have written about him in *Keeping Out, Letting Through*, the fourth volume of this series.

In March 1988 I wrote a letter asking to go to Copenhagen, to the European conference of SAS users. I wrote it and my head of unit signed it: at the bottom, under her signature, are the drafter's initials, GM. By then the terminal had become a personal computer, still connected to the central machine. The letter is an office letter, with a registry number and an arrival stamp, but it states precisely what five years had made of the work in which someone had seen a game:

> "The SAS package has proved an indispensable instrument for the automatic processing of information from archives created through systematic surveys, as well as through targeted epidemiological and economic enquiries."

I appear in it as the official who had "followed and coordinated the automatic data-processing activity, by means of SAS, since its installation in 1983 at the Health Department". Attendance cost three hundred Deutschmarks. I did go to Copenhagen, in April, and what stayed with me from that journey was above all a piece of music heard by chance in a museum. I have told that story in another book, *Dare Ascolto* (Listening), so far only in Italian; it is not needed here.

What is needed is to look a little further back, because the terminal had not been my first encounter with numbers. In the years when I worked at the Education Department I had carried out a study, with Giorgio Chiari, who taught the methodology of social research at the University of Trento, and Graziano Manfrini, a programmer skilled in SPSS, another statistical package, on the working lives of the province's school-leavers: what young people did after school, who found work, who went on studying. On 16 December 1980 the provincial minister for education, Claudia Piccoli, wrote to her colleague responsible for labour a letter I have found among my papers. (In Trentino, as in the Italian regions, the members of the executive are called *assessori*; in English they are best thought of as ministers of a small government.)

> "The data we collected and published in 'The Occupational Destiny of Diploma Holders and Qualified Workers' are 'ageing', and in my view they need updating as well as checking."

She proposed a working group between the two departments and a "periodic sample survey". Whether that group ever came into being, the papers do not say and I do not remember. The sentence remains: data age, and if nobody updates them they stop saying anything about the present. In that letter it was a minister who asked for it.

In 1984 it was a newspaper that reported a study. On 23 December, on its page for the Giudicarie valleys, the local daily *l'Adige* gave the results of a survey of the people using the psychiatric services of the area, which I had carried out with the psychiatrists Massimo Antonioli and Franco Garonna, six years after the law that had closed Italy's asylums. After tables and percentages, the article ended:

> "What the psychiatrists Antonioli and Garonna and the sociologist Martini have brought to light with such a wealth of data is no more than the X-ray of a problem, one that calls for new experiments in education and information and for the involvement of the local community."

The X-ray of a problem: the journalist had found the right image. An X-ray shows what is there, but does not decide what to do about it. For that you need someone who looks at it and takes responsibility for a treatment.

One measure, in those years, really worked. From 1 January 1979 a form with more than sixty items on the pregnancy, the delivery and the newborn was filled in for every child born in the province, and the forms reached the Health Department from every maternity ward. In March 1987, in Vienna, at a world symposium on the use of computers in the care of mother and newborn, I presented the results of eight years with the head of neonatology, Dino Pedrotti, and the neonatologist and epidemiologist Maurizio Coraiola: some 34,000 newborns, 99 per cent of the births in the province. It was a measure someone had asked for: the doctors who used it.

Set side by side, these episodes describe an apprenticeship. A sociologist who has entered a provincial administration learns that data can be asked for: from the system that produces them for other reasons, from the archives, from people with a questionnaire. He learns that they can be read, and that once read they say things nobody had foreseen. But they also describe something I did not see at the time. In almost every case the question came from a person: from a minister who did not want a study to grow old, from a curious doctor, from me. The institution produced the data, kept them, sometimes published them. Whether they were read, and whether anyone drew conclusions from them, depended on who happened to be there at the time.

Work with numbers in an office that never asked for them, and you look as if you are playing. The remark in my doorway was not unkind, and it left no bitterness in me. As a sociologist I rather wondered about the backwardness of the environment I worked in. I remember it today because it already contained, in miniature, the question from which this book was born. That official did not know what I was looking at because nobody in the office had asked me to look at it: the work on data was there, but nobody was waiting for it, and so nobody knew what to do with it. Twenty years later something similar would happen, on a much larger scale, to a measure concerning the health of an entire province.

That measure is what this book is about. Between 2002 and 2007 the unit I directed at the Province of Trento tried to see, systematically, something health data do not usually show: that people with less education and fewer resources fall ill and die more often, even in a rich, well-run province whose health indicators are among the best in Italy. First came a definition of equity, then a working group, then a monitoring system built by an epidemiologist working in London. The system produced a clear result and was published. It was never updated.

To understand how we got there we have to start further back. The first part of the book begins in Scotland, in the autumn of 1987, with a seminar at the University of Edinburgh called *Information and Decision-Making*. I went with a small group of officials from a few northern Italian regions, and there I met the sociologist who coordinated it, Erio Ziglio, a Trentino man who taught at that university. A few years later he moved to the World Health Organization, and our paths crossed again.

They crossed in 1994 and 1995, when the provinces of Trento and Bolzano carried out with the WHO Regional Office for Europe a project called *Investment in Health*. The Yale School of Public Health took part, and so did a London institute that advised public administrations. The project was meant to teach a way of thinking about health beyond the boundaries of the health service: it asked transport, schools and social services what they could do for people's health. Trento chose the ageing of the population as its theme. I coordinated the Trento team, and the final report, printed in Italian, English and German, already contained almost everything that would come later, including the obstacles we were going to meet.

The second part is about a journal, and beside the journal the health plans we wrote and that never reached the provincial parliament, the Council, for a vote. In 1995 I had the idea of circulating, inside Trentino's health system, the documents we were reading to prepare our planning: WHO papers, foreign reports, texts that did not exist in Italian. The first booklet came out in January 1996, printed by the Province's duplicating centre and marked "for internal use only". It was called Punto Omega, a name borrowed from Teilhard de Chardin, and from 1999 it became a real journal, with a title registered with the court and an editorial staff. Between 1999 and 2008 twenty-four issues appeared, and nearly two hundred people wrote for them.

The third part is the part of the measure: the definition of equity, the working group, the system for monitoring health inequalities. The fourth tells what came after 2007, when the unit I had directed no longer existed and I was retired: the laws, the plans, the observatories. And the way the word "inequalities" went on appearing in official documents while the instrument used to look at them changed, and with the instrument what could be seen.

The title of this book comes from a sentence in an official document of 2008, which the reader will meet towards the end. I will not quote it in advance: on its own it says more than any comment.

This is not a book of grievances. I look at those years from a distance, and calmly. What could be done then, I believe we did; what was missing did not depend on one person, nor on one political season. For that reason I will let the papers speak above all: reports, journals, letters, resolutions, laws. Many I had kept myself; others resurfaced in 2026 from the archives of the web, where they had lain for almost thirty years without anyone looking for them.

Papers, though, are written by people, and events cannot be separated from those who lived them. I have named people when the facts passed through them. Many others who worked with me in those years do not appear here: the book is theirs too.

The question that holds papers and people together is simple, and the book does not claim to close it: what happens to a measure nobody had asked for?

## Part One: Learning to Look

### Edinburgh, 1987

In September 1987 I belonged to a study and coordination group that the Italian Ministry of the Interior had set up for the information system of the social services: a handful of officials from a few northern regions, asked to think about what data to collect on social care, and how. With that group I went to Scotland, to attend a seminar at the University of Edinburgh.

What remains of it is a certificate on the letterhead of the Department of Social Policy and Social Work, dated 1 October 1987. It states that "Mr. Giovanni Martini" attended a four-day seminar, from 28 September to 1 October, organised jointly by that department and the Research Unit in Health and Behavioural Change. In Italy, where every graduate is a *dottore*, I was Dr Martini; in Edinburgh, where the title belongs to those who hold a doctorate, Mr was enough. The seminar had a title that reads today like a programme: *Information and Decision-Making*.

The pairing is less obvious than it looks. In an administration, the people who gather information and the people who take decisions are usually different people, in different offices, speaking different languages. The first produce tables; the second receive them when they have asked for them, and often they have not. An information system exists so that someone, sooner or later, will decide better; but the passage from the one to the other does not happen by itself, and a seminar that puts the two words in the same title starts from a question: how does a piece of data become a decision, and whose job is it to walk it from one office to the other?

The seminar was coordinated by a sociologist who taught in that department. The certificate bears two signatures: the head of department, Alex Robertson, and his, Erio Ziglio, "Lecturer in Social Policy" and research fellow of the unit. We discovered we had a good deal in common. He was from Trentino, he worked on health, and he had taken his degree in sociology at Trento, as I had, with Giorgio Chiari as his supervisor — the professor with whom, a few years earlier, I had carried out the study of school-leavers. In 1992 he would move to the WHO Regional Office for Europe, in Copenhagen.

It would be easy, at this point, to draw a line: the meeting of 1987, the project of 1994, a collaboration lasting thirty years. Things went less tidily. When, some years later, we found ourselves working together in Trento, it was by chance: the project had been agreed between the WHO, the provincial minister for health and my head of unit, and I had not conceived it. Edinburgh does not explain what came after. It only explains why, when we met again, we did not need introducing.

The title remains. Information and decisions are the two halves of everything this book tells, and the trouble is almost always where they meet.

### A problem to find

In November 1993, the final report says, the Autonomous Provinces of Trento and Bolzano "engaged the support of the World Health Organisation (WHO)" for a project that would help them think about how the structures, processes and resources under their control could produce more health for the population. It was the first of a series of demonstration projects that the WHO Regional Office for Europe called *Investment in Health*. In his foreword Erio Ziglio, by then working in Copenhagen, explains: "A legislated commitment by the two provinces made possible an officially authorized invitation to WHO/EURO and its collaborators, the Office for Public Management and the Yale School of Public Health".

The first contacts, and probably the first formal acts, were the work of the minister for health of the time, Erminio Lorenzini, who stayed in office until March 1994. The project was carried out under his successor, Pino Morandini. My head of unit, Gianluigi Bozza, was its point of reference for Trento; I took part in the meetings of the working group and conducted some of the interviews. In 1995 Bozza was called to direct the Cultural Activities unit, I was appointed acting head in his place, and the project passed to me. I tried to involve the whole unit that had been entrusted to me. A certificate of 17 June 1995, signed by Ziglio, states that I "served as coordinator of the operational team".

The machine was complex. At the top was a project team in which the WHO held the supervision, the Yale School of Public Health looked after data analysis and international comparison, and the Office for Public Management, a London institute advising public administrations, designed the process, led the groups and documented the work. In the provinces there was a steering group and two operational teams, one for Trento and one for Bolzano. A foundation based in Milan, the Fondazione Incontri di Madruzzo, acted as organisational go-between for the whole life of the project.

The method had a name that an English reader will recognise at once, and that in Italian sounds awkward: action learning. The idea, which goes back to Reg Revans, is that "managers and professionals learn best with and from each other by tackling real life issues rather than being taught or told how to do things". The experts from outside were not there to tell us what to do. The report puts it precisely: "The model is not one of 'doctor-patient' with the consultants telling the OTs what is wrong and how they need to change", the OTs being the operational teams. They were there to help us find out.

A problem to tackle, though, was something we had to have. And here the final report is frank in a way I still reread with a certain admiration:

> The "Investment in Health" project was not triggered by the need to tackle a difficult health problem. Nor was it motivated by concern for the level and nature of public expenditure and investment in health and health care. To some extent the Provinces of Trento and Bolzano had to 'find' a health issue which would enable them to learn about a new way of working. Moreover, as a relatively wealthy region in which a significant proportion of public sector expenditure is dedicated to health, resources to promote health and provide health care were not perceived to be in short supply. This clearly had an impact on people's interest in and motivation to become involved in the project.

That is how it was. In Trentino, in the mid-1990s, the problems that emerged later were not there: nearly everyone had a home, poverty was contained, immigration was in the background. The only problem in view, though in my opinion it was neglected, was the ageing of the population. Trento chose that, under the heading of promoting healthy ageing. Bolzano chose the prevention of injuries from accidents.

Wealth as an obstacle is an idea that returns in this book more than once. A province with resources does not feel the need to change the way it spends them, and a project asking it to do so starts at a disadvantage. The report said it in a line, about the motivation of the participants. Years later the same thought came back in other words: in 2004, at the end of the work on equity, I would meet it again under the name of "happy island".

The real work began with the interviews. The project asked us to step outside the health service and go and listen to the other sectors, the ones that have to do with health without knowing it or without saying so. I spoke with the commander of Trento's municipal police and with the head of the Province's civil protection service. The second I had known all my life, because as a young man he lived near me. I remember that both received me warmly and were very willing to help. In the slides I presented a few months later the figures for that phase are these: fourteen interviewers, forty-one people interviewed, two discussion groups with twenty participants, sixty hours of conversation. Out of it came a hundred and eighteen aspects of the problem, gathered into sixteen categories, and a hundred and forty-seven possible actions.

People's willingness, however, was not enough. The final report admits it plainly: the operational teams "were drawn primarily from the health sector", and "the overall project fell some way short of real dynamic intersectoral work". Working across sectors, it observes, "is counter-intuitive and counter-cultural. It is not career enhancing for those that struggle to make it happen". My two conversations had been cordial and useful. But no mechanism in the administration turned them into anything more than conversations.

In the spring of 1995 came the seminars. On 28 April Giovanni Bertin, of the University of Trento, gave one on the multi-criteria approach to choosing public policies: how to compare different actions when the criteria for judging them are many and cannot be reduced to one. On 8 and 9 June James Jekel, an epidemiologist, came from Yale for a seminar on interpreting data in the choice of public policies. Beside him, in the same team, was Lowell Levin, and from the Office for Public Management came Laurie McMahon, who led the groups.

The chapter of the report on data interpretation is, for me, the heart of the whole project. It opens with a domestic image: understanding a health problem "is akin to peeling away the layers of an onion — each layer getting nearer to the root cause of the problem". Then it warns that "if the real determinants of health are not identified there is a danger that any proposed actions may focus solely on the symptoms". And it offers a distinction I would have liked to hear twelve years earlier, in front of my terminal:

> Intelligence is similar to a story — it tells us something about what is happening to whom and why. Information provides the sentences and paragraphs, whilst data are the words and letters. Data alone is comparatively useless.

Three questions follow, always to be asked and in this order: what the data say, what they mean in this context, what can be concluded from them. And a sentence that holds in embryo everything the unit would try to do ten years later: "The real challenge is to develop an 'epidemiology' of health rather than illness."

In its closing assessment the report notes that the project had offered the operational teams "a valuable counterbalance to their traditional analyses which have been almost entirely quantitative". For someone who, like me, had spent the 1980s counting hospital admissions, this was a reversal. Numbers were not enough: they had to be read, and reading them also meant listening to people, collecting opinions, giving weight to what a table does not contain.

There was also, in Ziglio's foreword, a sentence on which few, I think, lingered at the time. The Health for All targets, he wrote, "are profoundly affected by broad social and economic circumstances, as shown by many epidemiological studies", and those circumstances depend in turn on policies concerning, among others, "education, income maintenance, housing, transport, communication, medical care, agriculture, and tourism". Among the limits of health services the report listed unequal access, and added: "Inequities exist in terms of socio-economic status, ethnicity, geography and gender." The criteria every investment in health had to meet included "equity, empowerment, accountability, sustainability". And the central principles of the project were the five areas of action of the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion, adopted in 1986: healthy public policy, supportive environments, community action, personal skills, the reorientation of health services.

By 1995, in short, almost everything had already been written. To choose among a hundred and forty-seven actions we needed criteria, and here Bertin's seminar on the multi-criteria approach proved its worth. As I told the conference in Bolzano a few months later, the group used two sets of criteria: those the WHO considered indispensable — equity, empowerment, accountability and sustainability — and others the group had given itself: working across several sectors, real need, acceptability, the balance of costs against effectiveness and of risks against effectiveness, flexibility, speed of implementation. In the end the Trento group proposed three lines of action: initiatives for older people, with cultural activities and mutual help; support so that older people with dementia could stay at home; changes to the urban environment to help older people keep their independence and get about. The report makes clear that, being a demonstration, the project "was not concerned with implementation". It was meant to teach a method. What would be done with it afterwards depended on others.

### Castel Mareccio

The project's steering group, which was supposed to accompany the work of the operational teams and keep it connected to political decisions, had grown and grown, because everyone with an interest in health asked to join. In the end it "numbered over 50 people". The report draws the consequence with its usual frankness: the meetings "tended to be formal occasions with little opportunity for open discussion". A body too big to argue can only ratify.

The last meeting of the steering group was held in January 1995, in Bolzano, in a castle with frescoed halls. My memory is that it was Castel Mareccio, in the centre of the town. Among the photographs of the project there is one of the Trento and Bolzano steering groups in a large room with painted walls and a long table, and I believe it is from that day.

At that meeting I showed a transparency, one of those clear sheets laid on an overhead projector to project them onto the wall. It asked whether the actions the Trento group had put together, under the heading of policies for healthier ageing, would be carried out; in the slides where I took it up again a few months later the question reads: "Will the actions be carried out?" To answer it I quoted a line from a Bob Dylan song of 1963:

> The answer is blowin' in the wind

It seemed to me the most honest way of saying how uncertain the outcome was.

The final report was written in the following months and is dated June 1995. The Province of Trento had it printed, on behalf of Bolzano as well, in Italian, English and German. Reading it today has a curious effect, because in the section on cross-sector work it lists the obstacles that work was going to meet, and they are exactly the ones the rest of this story met. Among others:

> Career structures that do not recognise or reward inter-sectoral work
>
> Rapid changes in organisation and politics and turnover of people which make continuity harder
>
> Data systems that focus on single sectors and communication processes that inhibit cross-sector working

Beside the obstacles, the remedies. The report recommended making sure that the idea of investment in health had "an identity beyond the key political leaders by securing the involvement and commitment of key community groups and opinion leaders", because at that level "there may be more stability" than in provincial or local government. It recommended political support "from politicians across the political spectrum — not just those from the ruling party"; and "firm plans in place so that any embryonic changes" arising from the project "are nurtured and sustained over the medium to longer term". Whoever wrote it knew how things go in administrations, and tried to say so in advance.

Among the report's tables there is also one that serves to judge the project at a distance of years. It is Box 8, a "checklist of measures of success". The report warns that the project's effectiveness "will be demonstrated in terms of the extent to which systems and process for inter-sectoral working to promote health are developed and sustained over a longer period of time". Among the measures: that "the development agenda for public policy reflects the principles for health promotion set out in the Ottawa Charter", that public and private policies are developed "with explicit regard to their potential impacts on health", that there are "new multi-sectoral committees with clear remits for addressing health issues" and "funding mechanisms which enable inter-sectoral investment in promoting health". It is a yardstick the project had set itself. I will come back to it at the end of the book.

The autumn of 1995 was busy. On 27 September the Province presented the draft Provincial Health Plan for 1996–1998 at a public conference. The speakers were Erio Ziglio, who came from the WHO; Pierluigi Morosini, an epidemiologist at the Istituto Superiore di Sanità, Italy's national institute of health; the minister, Pino Morandini; the director-general of the Health Department, Renzo Michelini; and I, by then head of the Health Planning and Research Unit. The Plan had been written over the summer.

Two weeks later, on 13 and 14 October, the Regions for Health Network, an initiative of the WHO Regional Office for Europe, held its third annual conference in Bolzano. It took place in the same castle as the January meeting, and I opened my talk by thanking our Bolzano colleagues "for kindly hosting us in this splendid castle". I said that for us the project had been "a moment of strong cultural discontinuity, above all in the perception and assessment of the needs of the population and of the health services". Then I told what had happened since the January transparency. The proceedings, which the WHO published the following year, summarise it like this:

> The Trento group sometimes wondered about the possibilities of carrying out the actions planned in the "Health Aging" project. In answering these questions, a line from a famous song of Bob Dylan "The answer is blowin' in the wind" came to mind. Since then, the wind has blown in the right direction. The Health Plan for 1996-1998 included, among the actions favoring the elderly, a plan of action for the improvement of the town environment to enhance their mobility.

It was the action on the urban environment, the one that by small changes was to make older people more independent in getting about. In the same talk I said that the new Plan and the new health laws were "permeated by the concepts worked out and discussed" during the project, and I showed the prototype of an Internet site on investment in health that my unit was preparing. I believed it, and I had good reasons to.

The proceedings also preserve the discussion. Participants from the other regions had learned that Trento and Bolzano were able to allocate a quarter of their budget to health, and they envied us: with such means, they asked, had we met other obstacles? The report gives my answer in a line:

> Dr Martini agreed that money sometimes allows people to be more confident than creative.

It was, said aloud, the same observation the report had made about resources nobody perceived as scarce. As an obstacle I pointed to "a reluctance to change on the part of the medical professions", the suspicion of many doctors towards anything outside health care, and I predicted that progress would be slow. The conclusions of the proceedings took up the point: the experience of Bolzano and Trento "demonstrated that money is not always a sufficient impetus for change".

The 1996–1998 Health Plan was approved by the provincial government and presented to the Council as a bill on 13 November 1995. A volume the Province itself published in 2022, reconstructing fifty years of Trentino's health policies, tells how it ended:

> "The draft Plan, however, would never begin its passage through the Council, partly as a result of the resignation of the provincial government that had proposed it."

The action for older people thus went into a Plan that was never debated. Today, at a distance, I would say the project had no political or social follow-through; of the three lines of action, I do not think any was carried out. The January transparency, with its line of Dylan, had given a better answer than the October speech.

## Part Two: Punto Omega

### The name comes from Teilhard

In September 2026, looking for the final report of the WHO project, which I no longer had in electronic form, I ended up in the archive that an American foundation, the Internet Archive, has been building for thirty years by photographing the pages of the web before they disappear. The service that lets you browse it is called the Wayback Machine. Inside was the first website of my unit, as it stood in 1998, hosted by a Trentino computing firm. In one folder there was a page with the titles of the report in three languages, *Garantire gli Investimenti nella Salute*, *Securing Investment in Health*, *Gesicherte Investierung in die Gesundheit*, and under each a link to download the files. The page had been archived on 21 February 1998. At the bottom, after the address of the Health Department, was a line: "Web page by Marco Mantovano".

Marco had real skill with computers and a keen interest in the Internet. He died young, a few years later. He was the one who had put those files online, in three formats for each language, so that anyone could open them with whatever program they had. It is thanks to that page that the 1995 report can still be read today: the rest of the Province's website from before 2009 has vanished, and with it a good part of what my unit had published.

The site had been born a few years earlier, out of an enthusiasm few people shared at the time. On 10 July 1995, recently made acting head, I wrote to the minister, Pino Morandini, with a copy to the director-general, Renzo Michelini, a letter I did not remember writing and found again among my papers. It began:

> "I am writing to put to you some thoughts on new telematic technologies — I refer in particular to electronic mail, access to databases and access to the Internet — which could make a significant contribution to an innovative way of working in health care, certainly more efficient and more effective."

I went on to explain that a health problem had kept me at home for a few days, and that I had nonetheless managed to stay in touch with my staff and with the Azienda sanitaria, the provincial health trust that runs the hospitals and local services. I had received, printed and commented on a number of documents that had reached me "by electronic mail [...] directly at home, at totally negligible cost (a very few minutes of telephone)". The Internet subscriptions we were using were paid for by a local health unit within an experimental project, and they were about to expire: I asked for them to be renewed out of a small part of a fund already allocated, and attached the draft resolution. To persuade the minister I had translated an article from the *Economist* of February that year. It told of a Swedish minister of culture, Margot Wallström, who went to Stockholm on Tuesdays and Thursdays and spent the rest of the week working from home, in the middle of the woods, with two small children, a fax, electronic mail and a teleconference on Monday mornings.

Reading it again, one feels how far behind we were thirty years ago, or how far we have come. In those same years a senior health official, back from a refresher course, told me: "They showed us electronic mail. Futuristic stuff." Twelve years after the remark about playing, the distance between those who used a tool and those who watched it from outside was the same.

We built the site outside the ordinary channels. The Province had a contract with its own computing company, which at the time was not able to build an Internet site, and we turned to the only Trentino firm that could. The prototype I had taken to Bolzano in October 1995, and showed during the lunch break, was the first piece of that site. As I remember it, it was the first website of the provincial administration. In the Wayback Machine the oldest pages on the server date from December 1997, and before that there is no trace: the date rests on memory and on the Bolzano prototype.

The same enthusiasm gave the journal its name. In 1995 I had the idea of a series of documents for Trentino's health system, and I was looking for a name. Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, Jesuit and palaeontologist, who died in 1955, had imagined that the evolution of the human species was converging towards a point, which he called Omega, at which minds would unite in a single network of thought. It seemed to me that nobody had anticipated the Internet and its promises better than he had. In the first booklet, of January 1996, Morandini's introduction as minister explains the name:

> "The series has been given an ambitious name, 'PuntOmega'. It refers to a concept in the work of Teilhard de Chardin which identifies a new and higher state of consciousness that would be realised in the community of human beings when all minds were able to interact in harmony, building, through a kind of network, a higher entity."

In the years of the new series every issue opened with a sentence signed by Teilhard. It comes from a page of 1924, *Mon univers*, which closes *The Future of Man*; in Norman Denny's English translation it reads:

> Forced against one another by the increase in their numbers and the multiplication of their interrelations — compressed together by the activation of a common force and the awareness of a common distress — the men of the future will form, in some way, but one single consciousness.

The version printed in the journal was freer. Beside the common distress it put hope, and to the single consciousness it added a shared knowledge: the men of tomorrow "will work towards the formation of a single consciousness and a shared knowledge". It was what the journal was for. And beneath it, two lines explaining that the journal wished to be "a meeting point for all those interested in health and the quality of life".

Things did not turn out quite like that. The web has multiplied the links, and shared knowledge has arrived together with its opposite; I have written about that in *La Piazza Privata* (The Private Square), another volume so far only in Italian, and I will not return to it here. But what I wanted then was clear. I had sat through many meetings with mayors, journalists, doctors and professionals of every kind, and had noticed how thin their culture of health was. The journal was meant to spread that culture and open Trentino to wider experience.

### Home-made booklets

The first booklet of Punto Omega is dated January 1996. It is a twenty-four-page pamphlet with a printed cover, and on the inside cover it declares its nature without any editorial ambition: "Promosan series", "edited by the Health Planning and Research Unit", "Printed by the Duplicating Centre of the Autonomous Province of Trento", and at the bottom, "For internal use only". It contains an English text, *Health and Social Care in 2010: A New Perspective from the United Kingdom*, written for the Regions for Health Network, in my translation of October 1995.

The minister's introduction explains why the series was born. In the second half of 1995 the Health Department had prepared the Provincial Health Plan for 1996–1998, consulting a "huge mass of bibliographical material", and now wanted to circulate at least part of it, also to offer "useful tools for the debate that will mark the work of the Provincial Council on the bill approving the Plan". The debate, as we have seen, never took place. The series born to accompany it outlived it by thirteen years.

We had prepared the first issues inside the unit. Nearly all were translations, and nearly all were documents of the World Health Organization that did not exist in Italian or circulated little: patients' rights in Europe, the Ljubljana Charter on reforming health care, the European Charter on Alcohol, the strategy of health for all, and then its version for the twenty-first century. The fourth issue was the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion, the document the WHO project had placed at the foundation of all its work. From then on anyone who wanted to read it in Italian found it there: in Trentino on paper, and later, when the booklets went online, anywhere in the world.

The second issue, *Patients' Rights in Europe*, collected part of the proceedings of a consultation the WHO had promoted in March 1994. On 16 January 1996, at the services conference of the Azienda sanitaria, I spoke about patients' rights in the Health Plan just presented, and pointed to that booklet as working material for the service charter the Azienda was preparing. In my talk I recalled that for centuries patients' rights had been written by doctors, from the Hippocratic oath to the declarations of the World Medical Association, as commitments made from above; now people were beginning to look at them from the point of view of those being treated. And I cited a Swiss study of 1993 that had gone round Europe, signed among others by Gianfranco Domenighetti. It had shown that the heaviest consumers of common operations, such as tonsillectomies or hysterectomies, were the least informed patients. Taking doctors as the benchmark, when they were the ones having the operation, the general population underwent 30 per cent more. The only group that came close to the doctors were lawyers, and the authors put forward a mischievous hypothesis: doctors are more cautious with patients who, if something goes wrong, know how to take them to court. Information, I concluded, can do a great deal for equity and for the health service's accounts.

The ninth issue, *For a Tobacco-Free Life*, I edited myself, and it was presented at a press conference with the minister for health of the time, Paola Vicini Conci. A newspaper cutting I have kept, without the name of the paper, describes it: the publication gathered "a series of official documents on the fight against smoking", from the charter that came out of the European conference in Madrid in 1988 to the ten strategies for a smoke-free Europe, and it could be consulted "on the Internet too" at the address of our site. I recalled that Trentino had already reached one of the targets the WHO had set for the year 2000, bringing smokers down to twenty per cent of the population, but that we could not "afford to rest on our laurels". In the same article the minister explained her point of view with a sentence I report here without comment, because it will come back later: "Every citizen must be responsible for their own health."

They were humble booklets, home-made, distributed to people working in health care. But as early as 1998 they could be read online, when few people in Trentino used the web.

### Cars and poisons

A journal that translates the WHO is one thing; saying the same things to a newspaper reader is another. In the years of the home-made booklets I tried, now and then, to do it.

On 26 September 1997 a conference on road accidents was held in Trento, from epidemiology to intervention strategies. My paper had a title that went beyond the theme: "From the prevention of road accidents to a new culture of mobility". It opened with a definition of health I preferred to the WHO's, taken from the introduction to Ivan Illich's *Limits to Medicine*: "In part at least, the health of a population depends on the way in which political actions condition the milieu and create those circumstances that favor self-reliance, autonomy, and dignity for all, particularly the weaker". I have written about Illich and his idea of counterproductivity in *The Threshold of Care*, the third volume of this series. Here it is enough to say that the paper tried to apply it to traffic. Accidents, I argued, are only the visible part of the damage cars do to health; then there are pollution, noise, stress. And I proposed a paradox:

> "To talk of reducing road accidents while accepting the continuous expansion of private transport is a kind of logical paradox, which can be illustrated by the wall on which someone had written: 'No more writing on walls'."

The conclusion fitted in a line: "We do not need to use the car better; we need to use it less."

A few days later I took up the same points in a letter to the daily *Alto Adige*, which printed it on an opinion page one Tuesday in October under the title "Cars and poisons". Italy had 532 cars for every thousand inhabitants, more than the United States, Germany, Spain or Denmark. Applied to Trentino, that figure gave some 245,000 cars, which lined up would have made a queue a thousand kilometres long and, stacked one on top of another, a tower three hundred and seventy kilometres high. The Trento city council was about to debate mobility, and I wrote that the news pleased me, because health and the quality of life can improve when health measures are joined with those of other sectors, "as, in our case, within the transport sector: this is real prevention!" I closed by hoping the council would make brave choices, like some cities in northern Europe, "so that people no longer say 'pushchairs never belong among the cars', but 'cars never belong among the pushchairs'".

Under the letter was the editor's reply, in italics:

> "Quite right. Only, Italian citizens often adopt the effective slogan you quote in their own way. Like this: 'Pushchairs go among the cars, and that's that.'"

He was right, and his reply still makes me smile.

The following year I wrote for the same paper an account of the Verona Initiative, a WHO programme which in October 1998 had brought more than a hundred European experts to Verona for the first of three annual meetings on investment in health. The article came out on 3 November under the title "Health, or complete well-being". It opened with a question — what does our health depend on? — and answered with an estimate that had been circulating for some time:

> "As early as the 1970s it was estimated that health was determined almost 75% by individual behaviour (diet, physical activity, control of stress and of harmful habits such as smoking) and by social behaviour (environment, places of living and working), while only about 15% depended on medical care. Against this, the health sector absorbed more than 90% of economic resources."

From the disproportion between that 15 per cent and that 90 per cent I drew the conclusion that the public sector should begin to "invest in health", identifying the areas of policy that create health: schools, work, transport, the environment, social policy.

I reread that estimate today with different eyes. Into that 75 per cent I put people's behaviour and the conditions in which they live and work, as if they were one thing. In the years that followed the weight would shift: social and economic conditions would move to the front, and behaviour would begin to look to me like what it often is, a consequence of them. It was not a conversion. It was a change of order, which can be followed in my writings of those years better than I can remember it.

### The consultant's consultants

The Health Department had a tradition: health plans were written by outside consultants. Even the reform law of 1993, Provincial Law no. 10, which had redesigned the provincial health service and divided the tasks between the Azienda sanitaria and the Department, had been written by a consultant. To put it into practice the Province had turned to a Milan consultancy, which was also to reorganise the Department's offices. Out of it came three units: health planning and research, health services, health economics. The first was the one I would direct from 1995, and one of its first tasks was precisely to draw up the Health Plan.

The minister of the time had agreed that the consultancy would include writing the Plan. So it was that in 1995 we became the consultant's consultants, and the result was the draft Plan for 1996–1998: the one presented in September with Ziglio and Morosini, and never debated in the Council.

In May 1996 a new provincial government took office, and health passed to Paola Vicini Conci. The Plan had to be redone. The director-general of the Department, Renzo Michelini, again thought of an outside consultant, and on 8 November he wrote to propose the collaboration of Fabrizio Oleari. I knew him well: he was a doctor who had worked for many years in Trentino's health services, at that moment he was running a health trust in Friuli, and I had co-signed several epidemiological studies with him. The letter named a fee of thirty million lire a year and asked me to prepare the act of appointment.

I did not prepare it. To justify an appointment of that kind, the resolution would have had to state that the Department lacked the competence to write the Plan. I informed Michelini officially that I would not sign such a resolution, and added that there was also a conflict of interest, since my name appeared beside Oleari's in several papers. The appointment went by another route.

The work, however, did not go as planned, and with the Christmas holidays approaching there was no Plan. On 27 December 1996 Michelini wrote to me again. He reminded me that writing the document was my unit's task, asked for a first draft by the middle of January, and said without beating about the bush: "we are in a situation that does not allow a second chance".

I took up the challenge. With Giulio Panizza, Monica Pisetta, Anna Delai and Graziano Manfrini — the same Manfrini with whom, more than fifteen years earlier, I had studied the fate of the school-leavers — we set to work. I spent the Christmas holidays at home, writing at the computer. When Michelini came back from his holidays, he found a bound copy of the Health Plan on his desk.

Then the Plan went its public way. On 18 March 1997 the minister presented its outline to the press, and the next day the *Alto Adige* published a photograph of the three of us at the same table, she, Michelini and I. A week later she explained it to the governing majority, and told *l'Adige*: "It took me nine months, less than Lorenzini." In April the provincial government endorsed its approach, and the minister announced that the Plan would also be put on the Internet. The page of our site presenting it is still in the Wayback Machine, archived in February 1998, with an invitation to download the text; the files themselves are not in the archive. In June a provincial conference closed a consultation that had covered the eleven health districts, with fifteen meetings and sixty hours of discussion. The weekly *Questotrentino*, which had awaited the Plan sceptically, reported the opinion of a general practitioner: it was "decidedly more cultivated than the others, with an interesting and rigorous general introduction". On 26 November 1997 the provincial government approved it as a bill. It never reached debate in the Council.

With the new legislature, in 1999, the people I worked with changed. The minister for social policy and health became Mario Magnani, a veterinary surgeon, and the director-general of the Department Giorgio Paolino, a sociologist. With them I worked far more serenely.

In the same year Carlo Favaretti became director-general of the Azienda sanitaria: a cultivated doctor of broad views and considerable organising ability. He had the merit of surrounding himself with very capable people of real worth: I remember Fabrizio Fontana, Paolo De Pieri and a dear friend, Alberto Betta, a public health and occupational physician. Relations with them were very good, and the discussion always of a high standard. I had met Favaretti in 1998, before he came to Trento, at the Verona Initiative: that is why I was the only head of unit in the Department on first-name terms with him. We are still often in touch today.

We prepared the new Plan by asking for proposals before writing: at the end of May 1999 from the district committees, then from the trade unions, the professional bodies, the patients' organisations, and even the other departments of the provincial government. The draft was put on the website, the minister met the district committees one by one, and on 23 March 2000 a provincial conference, held at the ITC-irst research institute at Povo, discussed it before about a hundred people. On 2 June the provincial government approved it as a bill, and the Council committee approved it in turn. The chamber never put it on the agenda; updated to 2002–2004, it went no further.

In that Plan, as in the 1997 one, there were explicit targets, with numbers, on the model of the WHO targets. The 2000 Plan aimed to reduce smokers by 20 per cent among men and 10 per cent among women, heavy drinkers and road deaths by 20 per cent, cancer mortality by 10 and 5 per cent. On inequalities, by contrast, no number. The 2000 Plan listed among its guiding lines "Reducing inequalities in health", but devoted to it neither a project nor a target. I believe that not even we, who were writing it, had yet come to think of inequalities as something to be measured.

It was the third Plan we had worked on in five years, and the third that did not become law. The frustration I felt then I can still recall. The last Plan actually approved remained the one for 1993–1995, promoted by Lorenzini as minister with Provincial Law no. 38 of 6 December 1993, formally still in force because nothing replaced it. Meanwhile Trentino's health service was governed by the objectives assigned each year to the Azienda sanitaria, and those objectives we wrote ourselves: a plan in miniature, redone every year, that had no need of the Council.

### A hundred and ninety-one signatures

In 1999, the same year Magnani and Paolino arrived, Vittorio Curzel joined my unit. I had worked with him years before at the Education Department: a man full of initiative, competent in culture and communication. In that setting a health communication project was born, and inside that project the new series of Punto Omega. It was run by a small group: Vittorio Curzel, who coordinated it, Attilio Pedenzini and Mario Querin, who took care of editing and design with skill and enthusiasm, with a decisive contribution from Giovanna Forti. Mario Querin was an eclectic character: besides his own work he sometimes gave me a hand with translations, and together we translated from English the second edition, of 2003, of *The Solid Facts*, the WHO booklet on the social determinants of health edited by Richard Wilkinson and Michael Marmot. It came out in 2006, as a supplement to Punto Omega, under the title *I determinanti sociali della salute. I fatti concreti*.

On 18 October 1999 Magnani, as minister, presented the new Trentino health website at a press conference: compared with the original one it had been substantially rebuilt. On 20 January 2000 the *Alto Adige* announced the journal:

> "The first scientific and popular journal of Trentino's health service is taking off. Its publisher of record is the minister for health, Mario Magnani, but it will be edited by the Province's health planning and research unit, directed by Dr Giovanni Martini."

The article quoted the minister: Punto Omega had been born in January 1996 as a collection of materials for internal use, and would now become "one of the instruments through which we intend to strengthen communication with the citizens who use the provincial health system". The colophon of the first issue of the new series, devoted to telemedicine, says the rest: "Journal of the Trentino Health Service, published three times a year", "New series, Year I/1999, number 1", "Registered at the Court of Trento, no. 1036 of 6.10.1999". Between the "internal use only" of 1996 and a title registered with the court, less than four years had passed.

The new series ran until December 2008: twenty-four issues in eighteen booklets, because some were double issues. Each was devoted to a single theme, and the themes say by themselves what the journal wanted to be: telemedicine, health promotion, organ donation, community care, the history of places of care in Trento, health in mountain regions, health research, childbirth in the small hospitals of the Alps, globalisation, cultures, communication. And, at a certain point, equity in health, which is the thread of the next part of this book.

One of those issues deserves a few more lines, because it shows how we reasoned. The United Nations had declared 2002 the International Year of Mountains, and in the Val di Non an initiative called the Mountain Project had organised a series of meetings on the many aspects of mountain life. The series closed at Rumo with a study day promoted by the Health Department: "Health and socio-economic development: challenges and opportunities for mountain regions". The question ran both ways: how the economic and social development of a valley affects the health of those who live there, and how health, in turn, can be a resource for that valley's economy. Erio Ziglio came from the WHO office in Venice to speak about investment in health as a condition of economic and social progress. Gianfranco Domenighetti arrived from Lugano at the wheel of his legendary black Cadillac, and spoke about the socio-economic determinants of health. Experiences came from other Alpine and border regions: from Carinthia, and from Pomurje, the most disadvantaged region of Slovenia, where a cross-sector project called Mura was trying to improve health and the economy together. An economist from the University of Trento, Luigi Mittone, spoke about small outlying hospitals, which in those very months were at the centre of national public debate, and about how they might be converted rather than closed. A round table then brought local administrators and practitioners to the same table with the minister and Ziglio.

The proceedings came out in 2003 in issue 11 of Punto Omega. In the article I wrote with Monica Pisetta, an able member of the unit's staff, to frame the day, the figures on ageing were those of 2000: in the province there were 40,500 people over seventy-four, 8.5 per cent of the population. The theme chosen in 1995 for the WHO project had become, seven years later, one of the threads of a reflection on the development of a whole territory. And at a conference of Trentino's health service the question was not only how to treat people, but what, in the life of a community, makes them ill or keeps them well.

In September 2026 I decided to count who had written for Punto Omega. Every booklet lists its authors on the back cover, under the heading "Contributors to this issue". Leaving out the 2008 review of the legislature, compiled by the offices, there are 239 signatures, belonging to 191 different people. Of these, 163 wrote only once. The name that recurs most is mine, in eight issues.

The figure that matters is the second. A hundred and ninety-one people — doctors, nurses, administrators, university teachers, experts from outside — and almost all of them once only: a network built one article at a time, by asking someone to write on a subject they knew. A journal like that does not make itself. It needed ideas and energy, people to involve and to persuade; it was not simply a publication, it was a journal that asked commitment of those who made it. Over time the communication project became an office, the Health Information and Communication Office, and it was that office that looked after the content, design and editing of the last issues.

The last three booklets came out in 2008, when my unit no longer existed and I had been retired for a year. The colophon of the December issue, the twenty-fourth, no longer names the minister as editor, probably because the legislature had just ended. For some issues, in any case, other names had been disappearing from the colophons. Until 2006 they named those who did the layout, Attilio Pedenzini and then Mario Querin; from the booklet printed in 2007 the design is simply "by the Unit", and then "by the Office". No more issues followed, and in the publications the Health Department has put on its website in the years since, the Office itself is gone.

## Part Three: The Measure

### A definition of equity

On 4 May 2002 a conference on equity, solidarity and the protection of vulnerable people in health and social services was held in Trento. My paper, "Equity in health and in health care", came out in August in issue 8-9 of Punto Omega, which collected the proceedings. I wanted to start from a definition, because with a word so easy to say and so hard to measure the risk is that everyone means something different by it.

I took the definition from Margaret Whitehead, a British researcher who had written it for the WHO Regional Office for Europe at the beginning of the 1990s, and I cited her in the bibliography. In her words:

> The term inequity as used in WHO documents refers to differences in health which are not only unnecessary and avoidable but, in addition, are considered unfair and unjust.

And a little further on, in the form that would accompany all the work of the following years: "Equity in health implies that ideally everyone should have a fair opportunity to attain their full health potential." The aim was not to make everyone equally healthy, which is impossible. It was to reduce the differences that arise from avoidable and unfair causes: those that depend on where one is born, on the work one does, on how long one was able to study.

Beside equity in health there was equity in health care, which is another thing. For health services the paper proposed Whitehead's three objectives: "equal access to available care for equal need; equal utilization for equal need; equal quality of care for all". The distinction looks like a subtlety, and is not. You can guarantee everyone the same access to a hospital and still have a population in which those who studied less fall ill sooner and die sooner. Equal access is the first of the three objectives of care; equity in health is a different measure, wider and harder. Later in this book the difference will come back.

Rereading that paper, one can also see where my way of looking had got to. On the one hand I wrote that among the causes of differences in health "social, economic and environmental factors count for much more, and lifestyles in particular". On the other, a few lines earlier, I had written that "personal choices about lifestyles that favour health can be heavily determined by specific social and economic conditions". A poor diet can depend on income, giving up exercise on lack of time, and advertising chooses its targets: alcohol among young workers, tobacco among girls. In 2002 lifestyles still counted for a great deal in my reasoning, but I was beginning to see them as the effect of something else.

The next step was a resolution. By resolution no. 113 of 24 January 2003 the provincial government set up "a working group, lasting one year, for the observation and monitoring of equity in health". I coordinated it. Besides my unit, its members came from the provincial social policy unit, the Azienda sanitaria, the University of Trento with an economist and a constitutional lawyer, the medical association, the Fondazione Zancan of Padua, which has been doing research on social policy for decades, and the Tribunal for Patients' Rights of Cittadinanzattiva, a citizens' organisation. We met once a month from February to July, and minutes were kept of every meeting.

The group also tried a new way of communicating: a newsgroup, a space on the web for sending notices of meetings, exchanging material and discussing between one meeting and the next. The final report is honest about it: the channel "was used to a limited extent, and communication took place largely through the more traditional means of paper mail and telephone contact". In 2003 futuristic stuff was still futuristic.

The group's work came out in 2004 in issue 14 of Punto Omega, *Equity in Health in Trentino*. It closed with some "Recommendations for continuing the work", addressed to what the report calls "a future group", which could "capitalise on the experience gained in the course of this year". And in the conclusions there was an observation I would meet again, in other words, in many later documents: more than 90 per cent of Trentino's people, according to a report of the provincial statistics office, considered themselves to be living on a "happy island". It was the same observation the 1995 report had made about provinces that did not perceive resources as scarce. On a happy island inequalities cannot be seen, because nobody goes looking for them.

The future group never came into being. For one year, by resolution, there had been a network of organisations around the measurement of inequalities: the university, the medical association, a research foundation, a citizens' association. When the year ended, the network dissolved with the group. When the monitoring system arrived a few years later, there was nothing around it any more.

Not all of what I knew about inequalities in those years came from books and specialist journals. Some of it came from a friendship. Gianfranco Domenighetti was a Swiss economist who for many years had directed the health division of the Canton of Ticino, and who had written a book whose title was already a thesis, *Il mercato della salute. Ignoranza o adeguatezza?* (The Health Market: Ignorance or Adequacy?). He had come to Trento for a seminar, and from then on we met many times, and in any case always kept in touch by e-mail. He wrote for Punto Omega in three issues, the first in 2000, with an article entitled "For a public health policy centred on the needs of the population and not on those of the services".

One day, at the end of a lunch, he lit a cigar. I said to him: "Gianfranco, you of all people, who talk about health promotion — and you smoke?" His answer was lapidary:

> "Socio-economic determinants first, then lifestyles."

In that sentence there was an order of priority, and everything the health policies of the following years would turn upside down.

### A consultant from London

In 2004, with the new legislature, the unit I directed changed its name and in part its tasks: from Health Planning and Research it became the Unit for Innovation and Training in Health. The guidelines on health policy the provincial government approved in November of that year named among the strategic objectives of Trentino's health service health promotion, communication, training and research, and much of this passed through us. There was the health communication project, with its campaigns; one of them, on safety at work, was called "If it isn't safe, what kind of work is it?". There was the training of health staff. There was the programme of targeted health research, to which Punto Omega devoted a whole issue in 2004, with projects such as the one, run with ITC-irst and the Azienda sanitaria, to link health data with the road accidents recorded by the police. And there was the journal. It was in this unit, in those same years, that the system for monitoring inequalities was born, and the project described in the next chapter.

Among the initiatives of those years there was one I cared about a great deal, the Dialogues on Bioethics and Biolaw. They were organised by Carlo Casonato, professor of constitutional law at the University of Trento and head of the BioLaw project, the same Casonato who in 2003 had been a member of the equity group. They were promoted jointly by the University's Faculty of Law, the medical association and the Health Department, through a scientific committee of which I was a member, and our unit always gave them all the support it could. The meetings were addressed to the whole world of the health professions, and beyond: in 2005 the first four were also opened to law students, who followed them as a workshop. That year, the third, there were seven days, all of them about health: what it is, borders and health-care federalism, globalisation, cultures as seen by society and by women, information, research. Between eighty and a hundred and twenty people attended each meeting. Instead of the usual evaluation questionnaire, participants were asked to write down a case from their own work: in 2005 the cases collected numbered 414, and a group of health workers and lawyers discussed them from the legal, ethical and professional points of view. The proceedings appeared in Punto Omega. At the dialogue on globalisation, in May 2005, Roberto De Vogli spoke on the right to health and economic inequalities; at the one on information, in October, I spoke on communication as a determinant of health. It was one of the few places where every part of the health service, and not only the health service, sat at the same table to think through real problems.

I met Roberto De Vogli through one of those chains of chance that, told afterwards, look like a design. Some time earlier he had written to me to ask whether there were opportunities for work in Trento: I do not remember whether he had seen our website or read one of our publications. He was a brilliant young epidemiologist, and had studied public health at the University of California, Los Angeles. Later I ran into him in Venice, where the WHO had a European office for investment for health and development, directed from 2002 by Erio Ziglio. I had gone to talk with Ziglio and with Lowell Levin — the same Levin of Yale and of the 1995 project — to organise the conference that was to be held at Rumo; De Vogli had come to talk with Levin. There we met in person.

A few years later De Vogli was in London, at University College, in the department of epidemiology and public health directed by Michael Marmot, the epidemiologist who also chaired the WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health, in whose work De Vogli took part. I put a challenge to him: to build something that until then had stayed on paper. The "Recommendations for continuing the work" of 2004 said that inequalities had to be measured; nobody had yet measured them. The Health Department gave him the assignment by a formal act, and the work began.

The method was simple to explain and ingenious to carry out. In Italy, the report observed, the analysis of inequalities remained "a sporadic and unsystematic activity", and there were no surveillance systems that took socio-economic factors into account. The solution was to link mortality data to the population census, which says a great deal about people's circumstances. De Vogli connected the two sources through place. He took the 2001 census data for each of the province's 4,471 census sections, the small areas into which the territory is divided for counting the population; he calculated for each the percentage of adults who had at least completed lower secondary school; and he compared those areas with mortality in the years 1999 to 2003, for a population of almost 477,000 people.

The report explains why such a system is needed in a sentence that, read today, is the most important in this whole book:

> "The main reason justifying the development of a system for the surveillance of health inequalities is precisely the need to develop policies and programmes to reduce them. Without such a system, it is impossible to know whether inequalities are decreasing or increasing over time, or whether they vary according to geographical area, group, or the specific health outcome considered."

The result was clear. In the areas where more than 80 per cent of adults had at least lower secondary schooling, there were 83 deaths for every 100 expected; in the areas where the share was below 60 per cent, there were 127 for every 100. The difference held at all ages, and it grew step by step: every step down the ladder of education brought a rise in mortality. The report commented:

> "It has been shown empirically that in the province of Trento there are important inequalities in health status, despite favourable economic characteristics (low economic inequality, low levels of poverty and unemployment), social characteristics (a high level of social cohesion), environmental characteristics (green spaces) and characteristics of access to health services. This result could not be taken for granted."

On the happy island, the inequalities were there. One only had to go and look for them.

De Vogli described the system as "efficient, inexpensive and very flexible", and set out the next steps: drawing mortality atlases by area, linking other data sources, among them hospital discharge records, and above all following inequalities over time. In that list I find the very records over which I had spent the 1980s, in front of a terminal. None of those steps was ever taken.

The report came out in issue 19-20 of Punto Omega, entitled *The System for Monitoring Health Inequalities in the Province of Trento*. The booklet is dated December 2006, but its print files are from October 2007. I believe the delay was due to the time the report had required, but I am not sure. When the booklet appeared, I had been retired for five months.

The issue opens with an editorial by the minister for health policy, Remo Andreolli. He acknowledges the result without circumlocution:

> "Trentino shows a situation in which the excess mortality in the areas with the highest concentration of people of low socio-economic status is statistically significant. Besides calling for further study and for monitoring of the situation [...] this fact suggests the need to do more, forming alliances with sectors outside health care."

Immediately after comes an article I had written with Diego Conforti, an official of my unit, "Health and its determinants". It contains a sentence that, set beside my article of 1998, measures the shift I spoke of:

> "Socio-economic factors (which also influence lifestyles) contribute 40–50%, the state and conditions of the environment 20–33%, genetic inheritance another 20–30%, and health services 10–15%."

In 1998 I had given 75 per cent to behaviour, individual and social, lumped together; in 2006 first place went to social and economic factors, and behaviour appeared in brackets, as their consequence. In the same article we wrote that the socio-economic gradient was the factor that, more than any other, explained how long people live, more than the contribution of health systems.

In 2007 De Vogli, David Gimeno, his colleague at University College, Conforti and I signed a short editorial in the *European Journal of Epidemiology* entitled "The pervasiveness of the socioeconomic gradient of health". It used the Trentino case for a general argument. Trentino, we wrote, "has been wealthy, healthy, egalitarian and socially cohesive for a long time and its policies should have had sufficient time to improve population health and reduce health inequalities. They certainly did the former, but not the latter." We observed that, on the data then available, smoking in Trentino did not follow the social ladder, so behaviour was not enough to explain the differences in mortality. The conclusion was that, "rather than being explained by downstream factors such as smoking, the gradient seems to be rooted in the upstream structural and institutional inequalities of society".

All this, however, was a measure. A measure is useful if someone uses it to decide, and to decide you need objectives: by how much you want to reduce a difference, by when, with what means. I do not recall that, in this area, the politicians I worked with ever set explicit objectives. My feeling at the time was that of the old Latin motto *quieta non movere*: let sleeping dogs lie. In any case, in those same years the health plans that should have set such objectives remained proposals, as we have seen.

The system was never updated. The project closed with De Vogli's report and a final meeting. The figure for 1999–2003 remains the only one: a single photograph, never retaken.

### Two projects in the same year

In the same months in which De Vogli was working on the monitoring system, my unit was following another project, born of an idea I had been turning over for some time: putting citizens' medical records on the Internet.

Until then digital health, which was still called medical telematics, had been designed around doctors: what doctors needed, whether doctors agreed. I proposed a Copernican revolution, which seemed obvious to me: a project for citizens — citizens, not patients. I was tired of seeing notices in hospital wards reminding patients to bring all their medical documents to follow-up appointments. I was tired of seeing citizens act as postmen for the health services.

The idea did not come from nowhere. Telemedicine in Trentino had had real pioneers. In 1996 Antonella Graiff, of the Istituto Trentino di Cultura, and Stefano Forti, who led the medical informatics and telemedicine unit at ITC-irst — Trento's institute for scientific and technological research, merged in 2007 into the Fondazione Bruno Kessler — had conceived and written a project called "Oncological teleconsultation and telecardiology in the community". It was submitted to the Ministry of Health, which judged it innovative and funded it for three years, and it was on that occasion that I met Stefano and Antonella. My unit followed the project on behalf of the Health Department, together with the Azienda sanitaria and ITC-irst.

The idea can be put in a few words: in a mountain province, with its population scattered through the valleys and many small hospitals, instead of making patients and doctors travel you make the information travel. Stefano and Antonella's group built what they called a virtual oncology clinic: a telematic network between hospitals, a digital oncology record, and the possibility for specialists to consult one another at a distance on a case, in real time or at different times. Until then, to discuss a patient with colleagues in Trento, doctors in the outlying hospitals had to leave their wards for hours, bring records and test results with them, and often bring the patient as well.

The project worked because two heads of department believed in it and joined it with conviction: Enzo Galligioni, head of medical oncology, and Marcello Disertori, head of cardiology, who coordinated the two departments of the Azienda sanitaria. Without convinced doctors, a health technology remains a prototype. Other experiments grew out of the teleconsultation: a website to follow the stages of the project, a European project on distance training and teleconsultation for the integrated management of cancer patients, a trial of teleconsultation in the care of people with Alzheimer's. Stefano and Antonella's group presented the work at conferences in London and Toulouse, and the Province chose to show it on its stand at the World Exposition in Hanover in 2000. The first issue of the new series of Punto Omega, in 1999, was devoted to telemedicine in Trentino, with articles by Galligioni, Disertori and the ITC-irst researchers. With Antonella I had written, the year before, an issue of the first series, *Beyond the Pillars of Hercules: Health, Health Care and New Technologies*, and in 2001 the three of us described the Trentino experience in a magazine, *Dynamic Air*.

TreC, though, was something else, and I understood it from how hard it was to explain. The telemedicine we knew, including that of our pioneers, connected doctors with doctors: the citizen was its beneficiary, not its protagonist. In the memorandum I wrote at the time, the problem was described like this:

> "At present there is no integrated record able to gather the health information relating to a person. There are paper documents of various kinds produced by different services, which only the diligent citizen collects and keeps in order, for the part that is handed to them."

The health services — the family doctor, the outpatient clinic, the hospital — each held a piece of a person's story, on paper or on systems that did not talk to one another. The only one who could hold it together was the citizen, if he or she was orderly enough to do so. TreC was to turn the situation round and put the information in the hands of the person, who would become, as one of the slides said, its "first manager".

It took me quite a while to get the idea across to Gianni Lazzari, who at ITC-irst had been made head of a research area that included telemedicine. Once he understood it, things moved fast. I talked about it with my minister, Remo Andreolli, and he with the minister for research and innovation, Gianluca Salvatori, whom he knew. We held a joint meeting, and I presented the project to the two ministers. We obtained funding, and Stefano Forti built the prototype.

The slides I presented on 22 September 2006 carry the heading of the Unit for Innovation and Training in Health. The project was called TreC, *Cartella Clinica del Cittadino*, the Citizen's Clinical Record: three Cs. Said aloud, the name made me think of the English *trek*, the long journey made in stages. One of the slides describes it:

> "It is a virtual place, available on the Internet, with rigorous guarantees of privacy, where the clinical information the citizen acquires from time to time through contact with the bodies providing health services can be collected, updated and kept, in an easy and guided form."

Another places it within a policy already decided: the initiative, besides being in line with European and national guidelines, "is consistent with objectives of the Autonomous Province of Trento's project 'The Information Society' (2004)". The last slide carried a sentence by Stewart Brand beside a 1987 painting by Carlo Preti called *Steamroller*. At the end of the 1960s, in California, Brand had created the *Whole Earth Catalog*, a fat catalogue of tools, books and ideas for people who wanted to live and work independently; many years later Steve Jobs would describe it as "sort of like Google in paperback form, 35 years before Google came along". The sentence read: "Once a new technology rolls over you, if you're not part of the steamroller, you're part of the road." It had become our slogan.

TreC went ahead. Born as an experiment from the collaboration of the Province, the Azienda sanitaria and the Fondazione Bruno Kessler, from 2012 it became a service for all the province's citizens. Today it is called TreC+ and it is an app, the door through which people in Trentino consult their electronic health record. Diego Conforti, who had worked with me on the monitoring system and on the 2007 editorial, followed its development with competence, passion and tenacity even after I had left: the same tenacity that had led him to earn a degree while working.

The two projects were born in the same unit, from the same person, in the same years. One has become a public service used every day; the other has remained a photograph. Why?

My answer, at a distance, is this. TreC was seen above all in technological terms, and technology in those years was becoming fashionable. It had two ministers on its side instead of one, funding, and a research institute that saw in it the chance to work on an interesting project. It also had a place within a provincial programme already approved, the one on the information society. The monitoring of inequalities had a consultant in London, a working group that had dissolved after a year, no written objective, and from 1 January 2007, as we shall see, not even a unit any more. It would have needed two things: culture and politics. Both were missing.

I believe that for a project to take off you need a series of conditions, or perhaps of coincidences. TreC had its conditions. The monitoring system did not.

## Part Four: After

### Twenty days

On the afternoon of 12 December 2006 the Scientific Committee for Targeted Health Research held its first meeting in Trento. It was chaired by the minister, Remo Andreolli, and its task was to prepare the research programme the Province would fund out of the health budget from 2007, for three years, with five hundred thousand euros a year. The bulletin that reported it the next day lists the members. There were the rector of the University, the president of the Istituto Trentino di Cultura, the director-general of the Azienda sanitaria, the president of the medical association and two experts from outside. And there were the two of us from the unit: "Giovanni Martini, head of the Unit for Innovation and Training in Health of the Autonomous Province of Trento", and Diego Conforti, an official of the same unit, "who will also act as secretary".

Twenty days later, on 1 January 2007, that unit no longer existed. Nobody had thought to tell me.

No act was devoted to abolishing it. In September 2026, when I looked for the resolution that had closed it, I did not find one, because there was none. The unit had disappeared inside a reorganisation of the whole provincial administration, decided by a regulation issued by the President of the Province on 31 October 2006, after the provincial government had approved its draft on 27 October. The regulation was registered with the Court of Auditors on 6 December and published in the Region's Official Bulletin on 19 December: a week after the Committee's first meeting. Two articles say everything that is needed:

> "The number of units is set at 56."

> "The provisions of this regulation take effect from 1 January 2007."

In an annex the regulation lists, department by department, the units and their tasks. The Health Policy Department has two: health economics and planning; organisation and quality of health services. Until 31 December there had been three. The functions of my unit appear at the bottom of the description of the second, after the rules on the Azienda sanitaria, the accreditation of facilities, pharmacies, inspections, the professions and staff:

> "studies, develops and carries out initiatives of health promotion, communication and information to citizens on health and the prevention of disease [...]; carries out research and study on citizens' access to health services and on epidemiological observation; promotes targeted health research initiatives"

The words inequalities, equity, determinants are not there.

This is how a function is closed without having to be named. There was no resolution abolishing the Unit for Innovation and Training in Health, which would have had to explain why. There was a regulation that redrew all the Province's units, and in the new list that unit was simply no longer there. To close an office you do not need an act concerning it: an act concerning all of them, in which it is missing, is enough.

In May 2007 I retired. I arranged a handover on my own initiative: I entrusted some tasks to Diego Conforti, above all the development of TreC. The people who worked with me moved to the unit that had taken over our responsibilities, but on some lines of work the interest, or perhaps the capacity, to carry on was lacking. The Punto Omega booklet with the monitoring system came out in the autumn. After 2007 nobody ever came to ask me how it had been built, or where the data were.

There is a difference worth noting, without forcing it. The Health Information and Communication Office, born of the 1999 project, had a name and someone in charge: it survived the reorganisation, inside the new unit, and brought out the last issues of Punto Omega. The monitoring of inequalities had no office. It had a head of unit about to retire, a consultant in London, and a report still to be published. The Dialogues on Bioethics and Biolaw also went on, because they had more than one promoter: in 2007 they were organised by the unit that had received our responsibilities, the University and three professional bodies, and the proceedings came out in the last issue of Punto Omega.

There are two possible explanations, and I leave both open. The first is that the measure lost the person who had wanted it in the very year it was published, and that without him nobody knew what to do with it. The second is that those who decided were not interested. The 1995 report had foreseen both: the "turnover of people which make continuity harder", and an identity that had not managed to reach "beyond the key political leaders".

### Worth a mention

In September 2008, at the end of the legislature, the Department published in issue 23 of Punto Omega its review of five years of health policy: *Health Policy and Health Activities in Trentino 2003–2008*. The colophon specifies that the issue "was compiled by the offices of the Health Policy Department". It is an orderly document, which goes through prevention, health promotion, research, hospitals, community services, and at the end lists the laws and resolutions passed.

On page 48, in the paragraph on community services, after the new pharmacy premises, the new dispensaries and the "new rules for the health assistance service for tourists", there is a line, the last before the page number:

> "Worth a mention is the study for the development of a system for the analysis and monitoring of health inequalities in the province of Trento by means of a number of epidemiological indicators."

That is all. There are no names of those who did it, no result, no follow-up. The line is not in the chapter on health promotion, nor in the one on research, which the issue also contains. It lies between the pharmacies and the tourists.

In fairness it must be said that in the same issue the words are not lacking. The Provincial Plan for Citizens' Health, prepared in those months, names among its principles "equity and the overcoming of inequalities in health, in access to and use of health services", speaks of the need to act "on the factors that create disparity (in particular the socio-economic and territorial determinants of health)", and lists among its priorities "differences in health status in relation to level of education and income". In 2008 the word was still there, and so was the recognition of the problem. What was no longer there was the instrument that had measured it.

There is also a detail the issue mentions in passing: the Provincial Plan for Citizens' Health, the one that named the overcoming of inequalities among its principles, was a "bill [...] approved by the provincial government in June 2008". The volume the Province published in 2022 on the history of its health policies tells what became of it: "this document too will fail to enter the Council's programme of work". This one too, because it had already happened three times: to the 1996–1998 Plan, to the 1997–1999 one and to the 2000–2002 one, later updated to 2002–2004. Meanwhile, the volume writes, health planning rested, "pending the adoption of the Plan", on the objectives assigned each year to the Azienda sanitaria. The next Plan actually adopted was the Health Plan for 2015–2025.

The fact concerns different governments and different majorities, and I attribute it to no one. But it explains an impression I had throughout those years: that on these matters politics did not set explicit objectives. The document that should have contained them did not, for twenty years, reach the Council.

### The instrument decides the shape

On 20 July 2007, two months after I retired, the provincial government approved resolution no. 1527, by which Trentino joined the trial of a national surveillance system called PASSI, *Progressi delle Aziende Sanitarie per la Salute in Italia*, progress of Italy's health trusts towards health. The news is also in issue 23, in a long list of resolutions.

PASSI had been born the year before, entrusted by the Ministry of Health to the Istituto Superiore di Sanità. It works through telephone interviews with a sample of residents aged 18 to 69, drawn at random from the health registers, and it asks how much physical activity they do, whether they smoke, how they eat, how much they drink, how they behave at the wheel, whether they have their blood pressure and cholesterol checked, whether they take part in screening. It also asks their level of education and whether they have difficulty making ends meet. It is a serious instrument, and every year it collects data nobody else collects. But by its nature it looks at behaviour.

From then on health inequalities in Trentino were seen above all through behaviour. In December 2012 the Province held a conference on health promotion. Erio Ziglio, from Venice, spoke about the social determinants and the WHO's new European strategy. Pirous Fateh-Moghadam, of the Province's Health Observatory, gave a paper on social inequalities and health in Trentino with a clear thesis: to increase the well-being of everyone, the most privileged included, "continuing to accumulate wealth is useless", and "existing wealth must be redistributed and a greater level of equity achieved". He cited Wilkinson and Pickett. But the data he could show were PASSI's: the association between economic difficulty and inactivity, weight, smoking, diabetes. The thesis concerned causes; the instrument available measured behaviour.

An outside study confirms the picture. In 2016 a group of researchers in public health at Sapienza University of Rome examined the nineteen prevention plans the Italian regions had drawn up for 2010–2013: of 702 projects, only 56 — 8 per cent — dealt specifically with health inequalities. Trento's plan had 32 projects; two of them addressed inequalities, 6.2 per cent.

In 2015 the Provincial Prevention Plan for 2015–2018 devoted a few pages to what it calls an *equity audit*. They must be read carefully, because they say true things. They recognise that the social determinants are considered "the causes of the causes of disease", and that their unequal distribution is "a question of social justice". They explain well why people with fewer resources smoke more and move less: money, time and information are lacking, and the stress of insecurity pushes towards smoking and alcohol. Then they measure inequality with PASSI data on smoking, inactivity, excess weight, alcohol, fruit and vegetables, and conclude:

> "This equity audit therefore shows the need to plan actions to mitigate inequalities above all on excess weight, cigarette smoking and physical inactivity."

The Plan also carries out an exercise: if the whole population were in the most favourable social position, Trentino would have 30,000 fewer overweight people, 17,000 fewer smokers, 3,500 fewer inactive people. Inequality has become a difference in behaviour, and intervention a correction of behaviour. That is the shape the instrument gave it.

For completeness, the same Plan also provides for an inter-departmental round table on health in all policies, and writes that health promotion "is not, and cannot be, the exclusive responsibility of the health and social services". It is the direct heir of what the WHO project had asked for in 1995. Its indicator of success, however, is the "existence and implementation" of a plan, and the Plan itself warns that the programme depends on national actions yet to be defined. What came of it, I do not know.

In 2017 the Observatory published a report entitled *Unequal Health in Trentino*. It is careful work, which discusses the social determinants, cites Michael Marmot and the international studies, and uses data from PASSI and from the school surveys. In its seventy-two pages there is no mention of the 2006 monitoring system, nor of De Vogli, nor of mortality by census section. The measure of ten years before had been neither refuted nor superseded. It had been forgotten.

This slide, from the social causes of inequalities to the behaviour of individuals, has a name in the public health literature: *lifestyle drift*. It was described by three British researchers, Jennie Popay, Margaret Whitehead and David Hunter, in an article of 2010, and Kate Pickett explains it in her latest book: "the tendency for policy initiatives to drift from the broad social determinants", the upstream factors, the causes of the causes, "to the downstream, individual lifestyle factors". The reasons are understandable. Measures on behaviour have more experimental evidence behind them, and the experts themselves, Pickett writes, judged them "more politically and socially 'feasible'" than the upstream economic policies they believed most likely to work. But there is a study that tells us how much those factors really weigh. In Whitehall, the London street where the ministries stand, thousands of British civil servants have been followed since 1967. They were all public employees, with secure jobs and the same health service; only their grade separated them. Michael Marmot, for many years the moving spirit of those studies, showed that mortality rose at every step of the hierarchy: the men in the lowest grade, the "messengers, doorkeepers, etc.", died at three times the rate of the administrators at the top. Wilkinson and Pickett report the result that matters here. The lower grades were more likely to be obese, to smoke, to have high blood pressure and to take little exercise, "but these risk factors explained only one-third of their increased risk of deaths from heart disease". An equity audit that looks only at risk factors sees, at best, a third of the phenomenon. The editorial De Vogli, Gimeno, Conforti and I had signed in 2007 in the *European Journal of Epidemiology* said the same thing with Trentino's data: the roots of the gradient lay further upstream.

In 1997, presenting the booklet on tobacco, the minister of the time had said: "Every citizen must be responsible for their own health." In 2006 the editorial of the issue on inequalities spoke of "a greater and more explicit responsibility of citizens for their own health". Neither sentence was wrong, and I do not quote them to reproach anyone. I quote them because they show that the lifestyle approach was already there, beside the rest. When the instrument that measured the rest fell silent, only that was left.

### Article 3

In 2010 the Province passed a new law on health protection, Provincial Law no. 16 of 23 July 2010. It is the law that still governs Trentino's health service today. Its Article 3 is headed "Health promotion", and in the text in force, after the only amendment it has received in fifteen years, the first paragraph reads:

> "Health promotion is a primary task of the provincial health service and of all those who contribute to it. It is carried out through information and training aimed at supporting the progressive creation of social and cultural contexts favourable to health and at inducing in citizens healthy and responsible behaviour, also through the promotion of physical education, in keeping with the provincial health plan."

The only amendment, of 2017, is the one that added physical education. The second paragraph recognises the role of voluntary associations that spread "the values of prevention, of care and of the timeliness of services".

It is worth setting it beside the Ottawa Charter, which in 1995 was the foundation of the WHO project and which the Province had translated and circulated in the fourth booklet of Punto Omega. The Charter names five areas of action: build healthy public policy in every sector, create supportive environments, strengthen community action, develop personal skills, reorient health services. Article 3 entrusts health promotion to the health service and carries it out through information and training. Of the five areas it keeps personal skills, gestures at environments with its "social and cultural contexts favourable to health", and leaves out the policies of the other sectors, which for Ottawa came first. In 2006, in the issue on inequalities, we had written, in the Charter's words, that health promotion "is not just the responsibility of the health sector". The law of four years later says it is its "primary task".

Then there is the faith in information. Wilkinson and Pickett report a study of middle-aged British women in which "84 per cent knew they should be eating five fruits and vegetables each day". Those at risk often know perfectly well what they ought to do. Telling them again achieves little if the conditions they live in stay the same.

The count of words says something too. In the text of the law in force the word "equity" appears three times, to be fair: among the principles of the health service ("universality, equity and solidarity"), among the criteria for funding, and in the tasks of the Observatory. The word "inequalities" never appears. Neither Ottawa nor intersectoral work appears. Lifestyles appear twice.

The Health Observatory provided for by the 2010 law was abolished in the following years and re-established at the end of 2024, with a new article, 13 bis. Its tasks are "monitoring the provincial health service in terms of quality, efficiency, clinical and organisational appropriateness and equity of access to the levels of health and social care", and analysing the health of the population. Equity of access: it is the first of the three objectives of care I had listed in 2002, equal access for equal need. It is not equity in health, the one by which everyone should have a fair opportunity to attain their full health potential. You can have the first without the second.

On the Province's website, at the end of September 2026, the page of the Health Observatory Office lists five functions. The first four concern monitoring the service, information flows for the Ministry, participation in national evaluation systems, and health profiles of the population. The last reads:

> "promotes, also in collaboration with the other provincial bodies, actions for health promotion and healthy lifestyles, the fight against inequalities and the promotion of gender health"

Inequalities are there, in the last item, among things to be fought, beside healthy lifestyles. None of the five functions asks for them to be measured.

I do not wish to be peremptory. But rereading it today, Article 3 seems to me written by someone with a much narrower idea of health promotion than Ottawa's, and who perhaps had never felt the need to set the two ideas side by side: the law's, in which health promotion is a task of the health services and passes through information to citizens, and Ottawa's, in which it concerns all public policies and the conditions in which people live and work. In 1995 I had noticed, in meetings with mayors, journalists and professionals, a thin culture of health. Thirty years later, a law gives me the same impression.

## Epilogue: Conditions, or Perhaps Coincidences

Kate Pickett, a British epidemiologist who with Richard Wilkinson has written some of the most important books on inequality, opens her latest book with a confession:

> I've lost count of the number of times I've heard someone assert that this report will make a difference, this report won't be left on a shelf. But they have been. I have copies of some of them on a shelf above my desk.

The problem, Pickett writes, is not only the "evidence gap": it is the "action gap", the distance between what is known and what is done. "The best possible blueprints for a good society are of no earthly use if they are never implemented."

She also tells the story of the most famous report of all, the one that in England started everything. In 1977 a Labour government asked a working group led by Sir Douglas Black to study inequalities in health. The report was ready in 1980 and concluded that the problem could not be addressed by health services but needed "radically improving the material conditions of life of poorer groups". In the meantime Margaret Thatcher had come to power. The report, Pickett writes, "was published in tiny numbers on a bank holiday", with a foreword by the Secretary of State that called the expenditure required unrealistic and made it "available for discussion, but without any commitment by the government to its proposals". The attempt to bury it caused a stir, and since then reports on health inequalities have followed one another in England: Margaret Whitehead's *The Health Divide* in 1987, the Acheson report in 1998, Michael Marmot's two reports in 2010 and 2020.

In the same years in which Trentino measured once, England did the thing Trentino never did: it set itself a target. In 2001, after a commitment made the year before, the Department of Health wrote it down: "by 2010 we will reduce by at least 10 per cent the gap in infant mortality between manual groups and the population as a whole", and the same for life expectancy between the fifth of areas where it was lowest and the rest. Clare Bambra, Julia Lynch and Katherine Smith, who have reconstructed the story, record that those targets were "widely criticised at the time for being unachievable and over ambitious", and that they "were, in fact, largely achieved". They also record that the gains "were easy to unravel when a new Conservative-led coalition came to office" in 2010. A measure with a target can work, then, and can still be undone. But first someone has to ask for it.

In Trentino there was no need to bury anything. The measure was published, acknowledged by the minister in an editorial, and then named, in 2008, with a mention between the pharmacies and the tourists. Nobody noticed, because nobody was waiting for it.

The 1995 report had asked to be judged "over a longer period of time", and had given itself a yardstick for the purpose, Box 8. Among its measures of success were that the agenda of public policy should reflect the principles of the Ottawa Charter; that the policies of other sectors should be developed with regard to their effect on health; that there should be new multi-sectoral committees with clear remits; that there should be funding mechanisms for investing in health together. Thirty years on, with the documents I have gathered, I can say little, and I say it with caution. The 2010 law does not mention Ottawa. A committee across sectors existed, for one year, in 2003; an inter-departmental round table has been provided for since 2015, and of its outcome I know nothing. Of shared funding mechanisms — budgets in which health and the other sectors, transport, schools, town planning, pooled resources for projects that would improve health — I have found no trace. I do not want to turn that table into a school report. It is enough for me to observe that the yardstick was there, written by the project itself, and that nobody ever picked it up again.

The report had also suggested how to protect whatever would be born of it: give the idea of investment in health an identity beyond the political leaders, by involving the community groups and the opinion leaders, because at that level things last longer than in administrations. Pickett, closing her book, gives an example of what that can mean. In Bradford, in the north of England, a poor city with the highest infant mortality in the United Kingdom and very little research capacity, an epidemiological study called *Born in Bradford* was set up. It was "funded on a shoestring and built from the ground up on public engagement and community conversations", and it persuaded 80 per cent of the families invited to take part, families who are seldom included in research. Today more than sixty thousand residents take part; it links data from health, schools and social services, and it has changed some of the city's policies: the age at which children born prematurely start school, early screening for autism. During the pandemic it became the scientific group advising the local authority.

The 2006 system was, in De Vogli's words, "efficient, inexpensive and very flexible". It had no community around it. The work on inequalities had had a network for one year, in 2003; the system, when it came, no longer had one. The Bradford measure has people who defend it outside the offices; the Trentino one, once its author had gone, had nobody.

On 26 September 2026, while I was gathering the papers for this book, *l'Adige* published on its front page an article by Erio Ziglio entitled "Social justice, a necessary good". Ziglio recalls that societies with good levels of equity and social cohesion have better health and longer life expectancy, cites Pickett's book, and asks what all this means for Trentino:

> "There are no simple answers. It depends on what Trentino we want for the present and for the future."

And further on:

> "What matters, though, is to have a systemic approach and a vision of Trentino that guide choices in health, economic, social and environmental policy and strengthen social justice."

It is the same idea as the 1995 report, whose foreword Ziglio signed; and I had met Ziglio in Edinburgh, thirty-nine years ago. I do not recall it to close a circle. I recall it because those words are still true, and still waiting to be put into practice.

The eighteen booklets of the new series of Punto Omega, the one published between 1999 and 2008, I had put in a safe place many years before. In 2009, when I sensed that the journal and its themes would have no future, I had prudently uploaded them to Scribd, a site where anyone can publish and share documents. In September 2026, having found them again, I deposited them, with the covers I scanned one by one, together with the 1995 report in Italian, English and German, in two public archives on the web, Zenodo and the Internet Archive, alongside Scribd, where the booklets had been since 2009. The report I rebuilt from the files Marco Mantovano had put online in 1998. Today anyone can read them, cite them, download them. The network I had in mind when I chose Teilhard's name, the one that would one day pool knowledge, has served at least for this. I do not consider it a vindication. It is a handover: the one nobody asked me for in 2007.

The question I started from remains: what happens to a measure nobody had asked for? This one was well made, published, acknowledged, and then forgotten, without anyone erasing it. Whether it went that way because the person who had wanted it left, or because those who decided were not interested, I cannot say with certainty, and I believe the two added up. I said it about TreC: for a project to take off you need conditions, or perhaps coincidences, and for this measure there were none.

I look at those years calmly. What could be done, we did, and what we did is still there, in the papers, available to whoever one day wants to take it up again. It is worth, at least, a mention.

## About the Author

Giovanni Martini is a sociologist living in Trentino, on the Pine' plateau in northern Italy. An omnivorous reader with a preference for speculative fiction, philosophy and science, he has always looked for connections between distant disciplines: physics and consciousness, East and West, myth and technology. He prefers unresolved tensions to easy syntheses. *Errancies* is the English counterpart of his Italian series *Collana Erranze*, and the place where he collects these border crossings.
