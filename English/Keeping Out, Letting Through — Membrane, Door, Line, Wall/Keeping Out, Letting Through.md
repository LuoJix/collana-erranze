## Prologue: A Passport to Go Home

On 4 March 1916, at the district office in Sankt Johann im Pongau, near Salzburg, a woman of thirty-four is issued a passport. On the document her name is Romana Rigotti. She was born Romana Gasperi, and in its sixteen pages that name never appears: Rigotti is her husband's surname, Massimo's, and the surname of the four children listed further on. The rest of the document describes her as the administration sees her — medium height, oval face, dark brown hair, no distinguishing marks. On the line for her birth the form asks for a year, not a date: it says 1882, and that she was born on 8 January is recorded in the parish register at Caldonazzo, not in the passport. Under occupation there is no job of her own, only her husband's once again: wife of a warehouse labourer. In the family it was always said that he worked on the railway as a navvy. The German of the form names the place, the dialect spoken at home named the work, and neither contradicts the other. Pasted inside the cover is a photograph, stamped so that it cannot be swapped, in which she sits with the four children around her. What is hers on the document meant to identify her comes down to the photograph, the signature and the colour of her hair.

Her husband, by contrast, does not even get a name: only his job. The name I have, because at home he was always Massimo, and everyone knew that in full it was Fortunato Massimo. That the baptismal register says Fortunato Massimino, one syllable longer, probably nobody knew. Between the name a paper assigns and the name a person answers to there is nearly always a gap, and nearly always nobody goes to see how wide it is.

The document is headed "In the name of His Majesty Franz Joseph I, Emperor of Austria, King of Bohemia and Apostolic King of Hungary". It bears the number 201, it is valid for a year, and on the line for the destination of the journey it states that the holder is travelling in Austria-Hungary. In other words, she needs a passport to move about inside the country of which she is a subject.

The four children are listed separately, each with an age beside the name: Remo nine, Aldo seven, Ida four, Dario eighteen months. In the column for place of birth, for all four, there is a single word: Trento. They were born in a city that has meanwhile become a fortress, and that in March 1916 they cannot reach without the permission of a military command.

Ida is my mother. Romana Rigotti is my grandmother, and that passport reached me as ten scans made in 2008.

Seven years earlier, in 1909, the ethnographer Arnold van Gennep had written a sentence that now reads like a prophecy in reverse:

> Except in the few countries where a passport is still in use, a person in these days may pass freely from one civilized region to another. The frontier, an imaginary line connecting milestones or stakes, is visible — in an exaggerated fashion — only on maps.

Van Gennep was telling his readers that border formalities belonged to the past, useful mainly for understanding the rites of the societies he studied. The historian John Torpey, who reconstructed the invention of the passport, agrees with the picture and corrects the forecast. In late nineteenth-century Europe controls had loosened almost to the point of vanishing, and it was the Great War that reversed the trend, for good. The documents came back as temporary measures, and stayed. Nor were they only for foreigners: the citizens of each state were put under closer documentary watch as well.

My grandmother's passport is one of those temporary measures. And it shows something maps do not: when a front opens, borders do not simply move, they multiply.

The first is the inner war zone, the *engeres Kriegsgebiet*. Entering it takes a separate authorisation with an expiry date: Romana's runs until 31 March 1916, and will be extended to 10 July. The printed text warns that the passport allows the holder to cross the limits of the zone only with the permission of the competent command. The second border is the ring of the fortress of Trento, and here the list becomes precise to the point of pedantry. A red stamp from the military command, dated 9 March, authorises passage at Lavis and at the checkpoints of Gardolo, Mattarello and Buco di Vela. A fourth name, Civezzano, is struck out in pen. On that day, there was no way through there.

They are the gates of the city where her children were born, counted one by one by a clerk who draws a line through the one that is shut.

The last stamp is the strangest to read today. It records that the holder entered the inner war zone at the border station of the Brenner, in July 1916. In 1916 the Brenner is already a border station, but the border it marks does not separate two states: it separates the war zone from the rest of the empire, and it runs inside the Tyrol. Three years later, under the Treaty of Saint-Germain, the same pass becomes the frontier between Italy and Austria. Some eighty years after that, with Schengen, the checks disappear. In 2016, while Europe argued about migrants, Austria made preparations to bring them back. The same place has served as a border three times, for three different reasons, across three generations of my family.

Because in my family nobody crossed the border. The border crossed us. All four of my grandparents were born Austrian subjects — at Revò in the Val di Non, at Borgo Sacco near Rovereto, at Ranzo in the Valle dei Laghi and at Caldonazzo in the Valsugana — and my parents were born Austrian too, my father at Revò and my mother in Trento, the city where they all eventually came to live. None of them moved from one state to another. In 1920, when the treaty came into force, they found themselves Italian. I was born in 1947, the first of the family to be born in Italy. The phrase for it already exists, and it comes from another frontier: the Mexicans who stayed on in the United States after 1848 put it in a sentence that has since become a slogan: *we didn't cross the border, the border crossed us.*

Three questions come out of this scene.

The first is what a border is for, if one is needed even by people who never leave their own state. The second is how many borders there really are, beyond the ones drawn on maps, and whether they are all alike. The third is what happens when one of them stops letting people through.

The answers this book attempts come in four parts, rising in scale. It begins at the edge of a cell, which a living thing does not receive ready-made but builds for itself, and which exists to choose what comes in. It moves to the door of a house, which separates and connects in the same gesture, and without which there would be no such figure as the guest. It reaches the line of a state, which declares who answers for what, and which starts doing damage when it claims to be a fact of nature. And it stops in front of a wall — a border that has ceased to be a relation between two sides and claims to be only one.

The argument of this book is not that borders are good. It is that they are necessary, and that being necessary does not make them just. A border can be drawn in many ways, and the same line can protect the people who live by it or crush them, depending on who draws it, what it lets through and who pays to cross it. Without a membrane there is no cell, without a door there is no guest, without a line there is nobody who answers for a territory. And at the same time, a border that closes completely kills those who try to cross it, and goes on being called a border only out of habit. The two claims stand together, and the book will not dissolve them. No border, nothing to cross; the wrong border, no crossing.

My grandmother, that 4 March, was thinking about none of this. She had four children between one and nine years old, a husband still working on the railway on the far side of the war zone, and sixteen pages of official paper telling her through which gates she could enter her own home.

## Part One: The Membrane, the Border That Chooses

### The edge that makes itself

The border of a state is drawn by someone standing outside the territory: a commission, a treaty, a surveyor with a theodolite. The border of a cell is not. Nobody puts it there. The cell makes it, and making it is the first thing it does.

Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela, two Chilean biologists, built a whole theory of the living on this observation. The word they gave it is *autopoiesis*, Greek for "self-production": an autopoietic system is one that continuously produces the components it is made of, its own boundary among them. The term earns its place because it says something ordinary words do not. The outline of a living thing is not a container it sits in; it is part of the work the living thing does in order to stay alive.

In *The Tree of Knowledge* they make the point with a precise comparison:

> this membranous boundary is not a product of cell metabolism in the way that fabric is the product of a fabric-making machine.

Cloth leaves the loom and then exists on its own. The membrane does not. It is produced by the network of reactions going on inside the cell, and it is at the same time the condition for that network to go on at all, because without an edge the molecules would drift off into the surrounding water and there would be no network. The two authors insist that these are not two moments:

> Note that these are not sequential processes, but two different aspects of a unitary phenomenon. It is not that first there is a boundary, then a dynamics, then a boundary, and so forth.

In their more theoretical book, *Autopoiesis and Cognition*, which they wrote in English, the formula is drier. An autopoietic machine, they write, "continuously generates and specifies its own organization through its operation as a system of production of its own components". And in Maturana's essay that opens the volume the edge gets its own sentence: "The organism ends at the boundary that its self-referring organization defines in the maintenance of its identity." Living things do not receive their boundaries, nor do they undergo them: they specify them, and they do it while keeping themselves alive.

Here is the first argument, and it is biological before it is political. A border is not an accessory of being alive; it is a condition of it. Life begins when something sets itself apart from the rest firmly enough to carry on, and it ends when that distinction gives way. Without the membrane's spatial arrangement, Maturana and Varela write, cell metabolism "would disintegrate in a molecular mess that would spread out all over and would not constitute a discrete unity such as a cell". Under the microscope there would be no individual any more, only a smear of material — nothing that could still be called a cell.

The same idea, in almost the same words, turns up somewhere unexpected. In 2010, in Tokyo, Régis Debray gave a lecture in defence of frontiers, a short text and a deliberately provocative one. To explain why a frontier is not a wall, he wrote that a living being can only form and grow by giving itself an insulating layer whose job is not to forbid exchange between an inside and an outside, but to regulate it. And he concluded:

> Pas d'insecte sans kératine, pas d'arbre sans écorce.
> *No insect without keratin, no tree without bark.*

The intuition this book starts from is already whole in that line: the edge of a living thing is there to regulate, not to forbid, and without that edge there is no living thing to protect. What follows is an attempt to test it — to see where it holds and where it breaks.

### Closed in order to exchange

If the living thing makes its own edge, then the edge is not a window. The conclusion sounds abstract, and its consequences are anything but, because it bears on how we think about perception, about communication, even about computers.

Maturana and Varela argue that the nervous system works as a closed network. Not in the sense of being isolated — the organism breathes, eats and is jostled all the time — but in the sense that its operations link up only with other operations of its own. In the theoretical book the sentence is flat:

> As a closed neuronal network the nervous system has no input or output.

And a few lines further on: "for the nervous system as a neuronal network there is no inside or outside". Someone watching from outside sees a stimulus going in and a behaviour coming out. From the system's point of view nothing informative arrives at all — only perturbations. Everything turns on the difference between the two words. A piece of information already carries its meaning; a perturbation is a knock to which the structure responds as best it can, and the response depends on the structure, not on the knock.

The experiment the two use to show it involves a frog. Take a tadpole, cut around the edge of the eye without touching the optic nerve, and rotate the eyeball through a hundred and eighty degrees; then let the animal complete its metamorphosis. As an adult, the frog with its rotated eye covered catches prey accurately. Cover the normal eye instead, and the tongue shoots out with an error of exactly a hundred and eighty degrees: if the prey is low and in front, the animal turns and flicks its tongue up and behind. And it does not learn. Repeat the test and the mistake is always the same.

The frog does not see a wrong world. It does exactly what its internal correlations prescribe, and those correlations are not a copy of the environment: they formed over the animal's history, and they go on holding after the anatomy has changed. The border between frog and fly is not a pane of glass through which an image passes. It is the place where incoming light stops being light and becomes activity of the nervous system — and what activity it becomes is settled by the structure of the frog, not by the fly.

Before going further, a misunderstanding has to be headed off, because almost everyone who meets this theory for the first time falls into it, and it is the very opposite of what the two authors say. Closed does not mean isolated. It does not mean that the outside world does not exist, nor that each of us lives in a private reality where one opinion is as good as another. It means something narrower: that the environment, when it touches a living thing, does not hand it instructions.

The distinction lies in two verbs, and the whole construction rests on them:

> the perturbations of the environment do not determine what happens to the living being; rather, it is the structure of the living being that determines what change occurs in it. This interaction is not instructive, for it does not determine what its effects are going to be. Therefore, we have used the expression "to trigger" an effect.

Triggering and determining. What comes from outside sets something off; what gets set off is decided by the structure of whatever receives it. A line later the formula is drier still: the changes are "brought about by the disturbing agent but determined by the structure of the disturbed system".

The example the two offer is the membrane we began with. Across the membrane of a cell there is a constant traffic of certain ions, sodium and calcium, which the cell takes into its own metabolic network. They are always those ions, and what settled it was the history of the cell's lineage, not a decision of the moment. Introduce different ions into the environment, caesium or lithium, and the cell does not treat them as a variation: the changes they trigger are not compatible with its staying alive. The environment offers, the structure chooses, and the choice has a history as long as the species.

The same goes for something we believe we receive ready-made, like colour. Maturana and Varela ask us to stop thinking that the colour of an object is determined by the properties of the light it reflects. The experience of a colour corresponds to a configuration of activity in the nervous system, and the same configuration can be triggered by different light — which is what happens when we see coloured shadows that the physics of light does not colour. What enters the eye is radiation of certain wavelengths. The green forms afterwards, as a state of the nervous system, which is why the same green can arise from different lights.

From here comes the term that keeps everything else from being misread, and it needs explaining because outside biology nobody uses it: *structural coupling*. Two systems that perturb each other repeatedly, and both go on existing as they do so, enter a history of mutual changes that fit together. It is not that one commands and the other obeys, nor that each keeps to itself. It is a history for two, and what makes it possible is that neither dissolves into the other. Adaptation, for Maturana and Varela, is precisely this, and not an organism's gradual approach to a given environment: it is the preservation of a compatibility between two parties that change together.

This is where the theory stops looking like the relativism it is often charged with. If every system decides for itself what to make of what it receives, it might seem that any response is as good as any other. But the cell that meets lithium instead of sodium is not interpreting differently. It is dying. Closure shelters nothing, and not every perturbation is compatible with staying alive. The edge chooses, and it can choose badly, or find itself facing something it cannot withstand.

What follows is a reformulation that holds for the whole book: closure is what makes exchange possible. A system without an edge exchanges nothing with anything, because there is nobody to do the exchanging; a system whose edge let everything through would be its own environment, and would vanish as a unity. Exchange needs two parties that remain two. And from this comes a criterion that will recur up to the last page. Where there is coupling, two parties change together and stay two; where one dictates and the other submits, what is left is no longer a border but a command.

The theory has an exposed flank, and its authors know it. In his preface to *Autopoiesis and Cognition*, in a section headed "In Contention", the British cybernetician Stafford Beer asks permission to enter the arena "where the angels fear to tread", and says he is quite sure of the answer: "yes, human societies *are* biological systems". Any cohesive social institution, he argues, is autopoietic, and he lists them: "firms and industries, schools and universities, clinics and hospitals, professional bodies, departments of state, and whole countries". The two authors do not follow him there. By Beer's own account they do not fully agree between themselves on the question, and decline to discuss it further. But the objection stays open, and it will return in Part Four, when the subject is institutions that close themselves off to survive at the expense of those outside. Beer, incidentally, is the same man who in Allende's Chile was working on Project Cybersyn with Fernando Flores, whom we shall meet a few pages on with a computer program.

Cybersyn was an attempt to govern a country's economy with the tools of cybernetics: a network of telex machines linking the state-run factories to a single computer and to a hexagonal control room in Santiago, with seven fibreglass swivel chairs arranged in a circle, where production data were meant to arrive almost in real time. Flores wrote to Beer in July 1971, and Beer reached Santiago that November; the project ended with the coup of 11 September 1973, and the military dismantled the room.

### The skin

The first border we feel is not a drawn line. It is an organ, and it weighs between three and four kilos.

In 1923 Freud wrote a sentence his followers would go on quoting for a century: the ego "is not merely a surface entity, but is itself the projection of a surface". Sixty years later the French psychoanalyst Didier Anzieu took it literally and built a concept on it, the *skin ego*: the idea that a small child pictures its own ego as a surface that contains, protects and sets apart, on the model of what it experiences of its skin.

Anzieu acknowledges a forerunner in Paul Federn, and in describing him says something this book could take as an epigraph. Federn "was concerned with boundaries. He thought of a boundary not as an obstacle or a barrier but as a condition that makes it possible for the psychical apparatus to establish differences inside itself". In Federn's view, Anzieu adds, "the boundaries of the Ego are perpetually changing".

The functions Anzieu assigns to the skin ego are several: to contain what stirs inside, to screen off the blows that come from outside, to hold together sensations arriving through different senses, to record traces. The one that matters here is individuation, and it is Anzieu himself who goes down to the level of the cell to explain it:

> The membrane of organic cells protects the cell's individuality by distinguishing between foreign bodies to which it bars entry and substances that are similar or complementary to it and to which admission or association are permitted.

Distinguishing, not repelling wholesale: the sentence has two halves, and the second matters as much as the first. A skin that was only a barrier would be a poor border. Skin is where warmth, pain, touch and water pass. At the edge of an organism there are no sentries, there are sensors: in the sentence of Maturana's quoted a few pages back, the one about the organism ending at its boundary, the very next words are "At this boundary there are sensors (the sensory surfaces) through which the organism interacts". The first border we have is the one that lets us feel.

Anthropology reaches the same figure by another route, and Part Two will come back to it: wherever an edge opens, culture lays down a rule. Not for what passes through, but for the bare fact that something is passing.

### The immune self

There is one system in the body that seems made for the military metaphor: it recognises, discriminates, attacks, remembers. We call it the immune system, and the vocabulary we tell its story in is the vocabulary of war — defences, invaders, barriers, surveillance.

That vocabulary has a date of birth. At the start of the twentieth century Paul Ehrlich coined the phrase *horror autotoxicus*, the horror of self-poisoning, for the improbability of an organism turning on itself. In the middle of the century Frank Macfarlane Burnet formulated the distinction that would be the pivot of immunology for decades: the system recognises *self* and *non-self*, its own and the foreign, tolerating the first and eliminating the second. There is a self, and there is a border protecting it.

The philosopher and immunologist Alfred Tauber devoted a book to this idea whose title is already a question: *The Immune Self: Theory or Metaphor?* The answer he gives, after three hundred pages of the history of the discipline, is blunt:

> I maintain that there is no such entity as the immune self as a definable scientific construct. Self is used as a convenient mode to allude to identity — a code for an experimental approach, or perhaps better, an operational construct.

It is not a dismissal. It is the observation that the border between own and foreign, looked at closely, is not a line anyone can draw once and for all. The body tolerates billions of bacteria that are useful to it, tolerates for nine months a foetus that is half foreign, and sometimes attacks its own cells.

Two of these facts deserve a closer look, because they do not weaken the line between own and foreign. They move it.

The first concerns the moment the line is drawn. In 1945 Ray Owen studied twin calves that had shared a blood supply in their mother's womb. Each carried inside it, and went on carrying as an adult, blood cells from its twin: cells genetically belonging to another individual, which its immune system never attacked. Had the same cells arrived later, from any other donor, they would have been rejected. From this observation Burnet and Frank Fenner drew the conclusion on which everything else was built: tolerance is not written in the genes. The body learns during development the marks of what is its own, and whatever it meets in that window of time becomes its own for good. The border between self and other depends, then, not only on what arrives but on when it arrived. The body naturalises by residence.

The second concerns the idea that the body attacks itself only when something has gone wrong. The immunologist Irun Cohen, quoted by Tauber, turns it upside down:

> auto-immunity is not an aberration, but is a property of all healthy immune systems.

There is no immune system, in other words, that never reacts to something of its own. Autoimmune disease is not the appearance of something absent from a healthy body; it is that same thing escaping regulation. The edge is never at peace, and health is not the absence of conflict at the edge. It is the management of it.

Tauber also shows the price of the military metaphor, and to do so he quotes Susan Sontag, who had attacked it when it was applied to AIDS:

> We are not being invaded. The body is not a battlefield. The ill are neither unavoidable casualties nor the enemy.

A wrong metaphor is not merely inaccurate. It decides how we look at the sick, and at whoever stands on the other side of the border.

The alternatives Tauber points to are two, and both lead back to the membrane. The first is network theory, developed among others by Francisco Varela, the same Varela of the cell: the immune system as a network that maintains itself, and whose first business is to preserve its own identity, not to attack. The second is phenomenological: the boundaries of an organism are not given, they can be described only in the collision with reality, and the self exists as a process.

The immune self, then, is not a wall around a ready-made identity. It is the continuous work by which an identity holds itself together while letting almost everything through.

### When information does not cross

So far the border that chooses has been a biological fact. But in the 1980s the thesis of closure left biology and entered a place no one would have expected: the design of computers.

In 1986 Terry Winograd and Fernando Flores published *Understanding Computers and Cognition*. Winograd was not just anyone. At the end of the 1960s he had written SHRDLU, one of the first programs able to hold a conversation in English. SHRDLU ran a small simulated world of cubes, pyramids and boxes of different colours: you could give it orders like "put the red block on the green pyramid", ask what lay underneath an object, have it explain why it had moved a block, and the program answered. It looked like proof that a machine could understand language, and Winograd was one of the bright hopes of artificial intelligence. The book is a retraction of his own earlier work. It argues that the road he had opened with SHRDLU does not lead where it seemed to, because understanding a sentence is not manipulating symbols but having a body, purposes and a history shared with the speaker, and none of that can be programmed.

The theoretical frame comes from three places: Heidegger, the speech-act theory of Austin and Searle, and a fourth chapter called "Cognition as a biological phenomenon", whose first section is headed "The closure of the nervous system". It is Maturana and Varela, carried into computer science. The conclusion the authors draw is that a computer manipulates symbols but does not receive meanings: meaning lies in the practices of the people who use the machine. However finely the grammatical analysis of sentences is tuned, only words and rules get inside the program. Sense stays outside, because it is not in the words but in what people do with them.

From Heidegger they take the idea of *breakdown*: you do not see the hammer while it works; you see it when it breaks. The observation holds for every border. As long as it lets things through, nobody notices it. It becomes visible the moment somebody is stopped.

Speech acts deserve a pause, because without them the program we are about to meet looks like an office form, when it is applied theory.

The philosopher J. L. Austin had noticed that part of what we say does not describe the world but changes it. He called performatives those utterances that do not report a state of affairs but are in themselves an act: promising, threatening, naming. "I now pronounce you man and wife" is neither true nor false, and nor is "get me a hamburger". Of sentences like these it makes no sense to ask whether they are true, only whether they are felicitous — suited to the context in which they are said. John Searle, his student, set out the conditions — felicity conditions, as they are called. He also showed that the matter concerns not a special class of sentences but all of them, and sorted them into five kinds: asserting something, trying to get the listener to do something, committing oneself to do something, expressing a state of mind, and declaring — making what is said coincide with reality by the very fact of saying it. The explicit verb is not needed: "I'll be there" is as much a promise as "I promise I'll be there".

Why this concerns borders is plain at once. If words were containers of information, a conversation would be a transfer, and understanding each other would mean receiving intact what the other had sent. If speaking is doing, a conversation is a sequence of commitments between two parties who remain two, and each answers for what it has promised. Nothing crosses the edge. There are two parties binding each other, and neither receives a finished content from the other.

Towards the end of the book, in the chapter on management and conversation, the two authors describe a program they were building on exactly those foundations, and they call it a "coordinator". It did not pretend that the computer understood: "We are not proposing that a computer can 'understand' speech acts", they write; the aim is "to make the user aware of this structure". It asked the user to declare what they were doing with their words. The program classified every message as an act: a request, a promise, a refusal, a counter-offer, a declaration of completion. At its heart was the diagram drawn earlier in the book, "the basic conversation for action": numbered states and the arrows linking them, with four possible endings, two of them ways of failing. A conversation, the authors write, "is like a dance, giving some initiative to each partner in a specific sequence".

Here a memory of my own comes in, and it serves to say what that theory seemed to promise to anyone working on organisations.

In 1989 I had been at the Health Department of the Province of Trento for six years, and that year I was put in charge of the Health Information System. It was Gino Colazzo, who taught computer science at the University of Trento, who told me about the Coordinator, and I think the floppy disk holding a demonstration of how it worked was his: a diagram coming alive on the screen, circles and arrows, requests turning into promises. I bought Winograd and Flores's book to understand what lay beneath it, and what I found there was not an office technique. It was a theory of knowledge. I went all the way to Milan, to Elea, the Olivetti group's training and consultancy company, to ask how we could acquire the program. I remember the air in those offices: it was the air of a company packing up. Those were the years when Olivetti was starting to lose ground in computers, and before long it would stop making them and become a telephone company; the people who worked there felt it before the newspapers said so. Nothing came of the Coordinator, at any rate. We never acquired it, and the program never entered the Province. The book was lost in one of my house moves.

What had fascinated me was the philosophy underneath the program. And that is exactly where critics attacked it. In 1994 Lucy Suchman wrote an article about it called "Do Categories Have Politics?" Forcing a person to declare whether a sentence is a request or a promise means drawing a border inside a conversation, and borders, as we shall see, are never drawn by accident. The book that explained why conversation cannot be boxed had produced a program that boxed it.

The intuition survives, though, and it is not a small one. Winograd and Flores say that information does not cross the boundaries of a system. What passes is a signal — an occasion: a voice, a line on a screen, a sheet of paper. Meaning does not travel with the signal. The receiver builds it, out of what they know and what they expect. It is the closure of the cell, transferred to a network of offices.

Something is still missing. An edge that chooses is already a great deal, but it is not enough to explain hospitality — the situation in which someone who does not belong to the house is let in for a while. That takes an object the cell does not have and human beings invented: a door.

## Part Two: The Door, the Border That Opens

### Bridge and door

In 1909 Georg Simmel wrote an essay of a few pages about two objects nobody usually puts together, the bridge and the door, and drew from them one of the most useful ideas we have about borders.

His starting point is that separating and connecting are the same operation seen from two sides. Two banks become two banks only when someone thinks of them as separate, and someone thinks of them that way only by imagining them joined. The bridge does not link two things that were already distinct; it distinguishes them in the act of linking them. The door does the same thing in a tighter form, because it is a separation that stays reversible. A wall divides, full stop. A door divides and keeps open the possibility of not dividing.

Simmel says it in two sentences worth quoting together, because the second is quoted everywhere and the first almost never:

> Exactly because the door can be opened, its being shut gives a feeling of being shut out, that is stronger than the feeling emanating from just a solid wall. The wall is silent but the door speaks.

A shut door closes more firmly than a wall because it has been chosen. A wall decides nothing; it is simply there. A shut door is a decision taken just now by someone who could open it again, and so it says something. It speaks, precisely.

The essay ends with a formula that could go on the cover of this book:

> the human being is the being with limits who has no limits.

We are the animals that draw borders everywhere, and for that very reason the only ones who can always cross them. The two facts do not contradict each other; they hold each other up. There is no beyond without a border.

That a bridge can contain a door is more than an image. Piero Zanini, in a book on the meanings of the border to which this one owes a great deal, recalls Ivo Andrić's bridge over the Drina at Višegrad. Halfway across, the roadway widens into two terraces. The one on the right, coming from the town, is called the sofa, and is lined with seats of pale stone; the one on the left is identical but empty, and there a coffee-maker has set up shop, with his pots and his brazier always lit, and sends a boy across the road to serve coffee to the customers on the sofa. It is where children play, merchants lay out their goods, travellers stop, weddings and funerals pass. The novel calls it the *kapia* — the Turkish word for a gate. A gate in the middle of a bridge: a threshold built right in the middle of a crossing, the place where the town stops to watch who arrives and who leaves.

### The guest needs a door

In a house without walls nobody can be welcomed. One can only be invaded, or go unnoticed. Hospitality begins where there is a door to knock on, and that is the paradox from which the modern philosophy of hospitality starts.

In 1795, in the third definitive article of *Perpetual Peace*, Kant writes that "the rights of men, as citizens of the world, shall be limited to the conditions of universal hospitality". The sentence sounds broad and generous. The lines that follow are of an almost cold sobriety, and that is where their value lies:

> We are speaking here, as in the previous articles, not of philanthropy, but of right; and in this sphere hospitality signifies the claim of a stranger entering foreign territory to be treated by its owner without hostility. The latter may send him away again, if this can be done without causing his death; but, so long as he conducts himself peaceably, he must not be treated as an enemy. It is not a right to be treated as a guest to which the stranger can lay claim [...] but he has a right of visitation.

Kant separates two things that languages tend to run together: the right to be a guest, which requires an agreement and which no stranger can demand, and the right of visit, which belongs to everyone. The ground for it is not kindness. It is that the earth is a globe, on which, Kant writes, "we cannot be infinitely scattered, and must in the end reconcile ourselves to existence side by side". The argument comes from the shape of the planet before it comes from morality: the surface of the earth is finite and closed on itself, so nobody can leave for good, and whoever arrives comes from a place that in turn belongs to someone. There is no elsewhere to send other people to, and for a book about borders that is the most solid argument there is.

In the same passage there is a clause of a few words that is worth a whole chapter: the stranger may be sent away "if this can be done without causing his death". It is not a ban on turning people back. It is a ban on turning them back into danger. A century and a half before international law called it *non-refoulement*, Kant had written it into a subordinate clause. And a few lines later he condemns the inhospitality of the inhabitants of certain coasts, who go "plundering ships in neighbouring seas or making slaves of shipwrecked mariners". Part Four will come back to it, when the sea has no gates.

Hospitality, though, has never been a private matter between whoever knocks and whoever opens. There is always a third party, and the third party is the state.

In the summer of 1981 we went to Budapest, guests of the parents of Ildikó, a Hungarian friend who lives in Italy. In Prague, three years earlier, we had not had to register anywhere: the hotel did it, and the check stayed invisible, like a service included in the price. In Hungary we were in a private house, and so it fell to us. We went to the police station to register, and what remains of that morning is the waiting room: dingy, with wooden benches. The door of the house had opened, but to stay inside you had to queue at a counter.

This is where Kant's argument wears thin. The right of visit exists, but the visitor is counted all the same, and the one doing the counting is not the host.

In my family, though, hospitality was never a theoretical question. For three generations it was how people survived, and each time the door belonged to someone else.

In 1915 my grandmother, evacuated with four children, was put up in a village near Salzburg among people whose language she did not speak. In 1943, twenty-eight years later, she had to leave Trento a second time, and this time she chose the destination herself: Caldonazzo, the village where she was born, with her husband and her daughter Ida, my mother, as guests of her brothers, who had a farm in the countryside. When the war was over and my father came home, the family found a place in a small villa in the middle of Caldonazzo that belonged to friends.

In that villa, in 1947, I was born. We stayed three years, then moved to Trento. The house no longer exists, and of those years one thing alone remains with me: an enormous wisteria on the front, and its scent, which was overpowering.

The first smell of my life comes from someone else's house. This is the point at which hospitality stops being a chapter in the philosophy of law: someone opens a door, and on the other side a family begins.

### Dirt is matter out of place

A border can be defended with weapons, with documents, or with disgust. The last is the cheapest, because it needs neither soldiers nor offices: it is enough for people to feel revulsion in the right place.

Mary Douglas, in *Purity and Danger*, starts from a minimal definition: dirt is matter out of place. Shoes are not dirty in themselves; they become dirty on the kitchen table. Food is not dirty on the plate; it is dirty on the floor. From there comes the sentence that made the book's fortune:

> Where there is dirt there is system. Dirt is the by-product of a systematic ordering and classification of matter.

It follows that a culture's rules of purity are the map of its borders. If you want to know where a society draws its lines, look at what disgusts it. And since those lines are written down nowhere, disgust holds them in place better than any regulation: a regulation can be appealed, nausea cannot.

Douglas also shows that the body works as a small-scale model of society, and that the points most loaded with rules are the ones where the edge opens — the orifices, what goes in and what comes out. It is the same place Part One called skin, seen from the other end of the microscope. A rule watches over every opening of the body, not because it is dangerous but because it is an opening.

The political use of all this is ancient and has lost none of its shine. The words used for people who arrive, when the aim is to keep them out, are nearly always words of contamination: invasion, wave, infiltration, flood. They do not describe a danger; they place one. They put the other out of place, and from then on the border defends itself.

### Passing is never free

In 1909, the same year as Simmel's essay, van Gennep published *The Rites of Passage*, the book that gave the social sciences one of their few truly simple ideas: the important passages of life all have the same three-part form — separation, margin, incorporation. You detach yourself from one state, spend a while in a condition that is neither one thing nor the other, and enter the new one.

What everyone forgets is where van Gennep found that structure. Not in birth, not in marriage, not in death: in the crossing of a territorial border, which is the second chapter of the book and comes before all the rest. The founder of the anthropology of transitions begins with frontiers, neutral zones and doors.

He describes territories separated by strips that belong to nobody, usually deserts, marshes or forests, where everyone has the right to pass and to hunt. And he observes that whoever enters such a strip is, for a while, in a special situation: "he wavers between two worlds". It is there, in that geographical suspension, that the concept of transition is born, to be applied afterwards to initiations and mourning.

Then van Gennep brings the scale down, and the sentence deserves to be read twice:

> The neutral zone shrinks progressively till it ceases to exist except as a simple stone, a beam, or a threshold.

This book climbs the scale, from cell to wall; he descends it, from the territory to the stone under the front door. The two meet halfway, and at the meeting point stands the same figure: "the door is the boundary between the foreign and domestic worlds", and so "to cross the threshold is to unite oneself with a new world".

The neutral zone, however, has not disappeared as van Gennep thought it would. The geographer Alastair Bonnett went looking for the greatest documented distance between the border posts of two neighbouring countries, and found it on a road between Senegal and Guinea: twenty-seven kilometres between the office where you leave one country and the office where you enter the other. Twenty-seven kilometres in which you are neither here nor there, covered by car. The observation he draws from it holds for this whole book: "Such in-between places remind us how dependent we are on borders: that our sense of order and certainty draws deeply from the knowledge that we are in governed territory." Van Gennep's margin, which in rites lasts a few hours, there lasts half an hour of dirt road, and produces the same effect: a suspension in which nobody knows whom to turn to if something happens.

The practical moral is in the title of this section. No culture has ever treated a crossing as a neutral fact: where you pass, you pay. You pay with a rite, with a wait, with a trial, with a document, with a sum of money to change. Mary Douglas, who learned it from van Gennep — "He saw society as a house with rooms and corridors in which passage from one to another is dangerous" — adds the reason for the fear that goes with the payment:

> Danger lies in transitional states, simply because transition is neither one state nor the next, it is undefinable.

Someone in the middle of passing cannot be classified, and a society that does not know where to put you keeps an eye on you.

My grandmother's passport, in the Prologue, is exactly this: the proof that by 1916 the neutral strip between Salzburg and Trento had become a corridor of offices, with revenue stamps and rubber stamps. Seven years earlier van Gennep had written that such formalities were a thing of the past.

### The space of misunderstanding

At a border people understand each other badly. It is an observation before it is a thesis: languages mix, words come back mangled, and everyone translates the other with the categories of home. Zanini gives this a whole chapter, and argues that misunderstanding is not an accident of the border but the way it works. People live side by side without fully understanding each other, and the not fully understanding is what allows them to remain two.

His strongest example is the most uncomfortable: the ghetto. The first, in Venice in 1516, was born as an instrument of segregation, a quarter closed by high walls with a few gates. But Zanini notes what Jewish history has always known: born as an instrument of offence, the ghetto worked for those shut inside it as a space of defence, and managed to turn segregation into preservation.

The same gate, locked from outside, is a prison; locked from inside, it is a community holding itself together. The wood and the hinges are identical: what changes is who holds the key. It is the clearest proof that a border is judged not by its form but by the relation it sets up — and it is also the point where the argument of this book risks becoming comfortable, because a segregation is not redeemed by the fact that those besieged draw an identity from it.

Of misunderstanding I have an exact memory, and it is the night I understood that a border can work even without a law.

Summer 1981, half an hour before midnight, the border between Austria and Hungary. We were tired and could think only of sleep. The guards took our passports, disappeared for a long time, and came back saying we would have to change some money. Ildikó got out of the car and went into the hut to talk to the officer in charge. From outside we could hear voices speaking Hungarian, heatedly, without understanding a word. Then she came back, and we were let through after changing a token sum. She told us she had asked them under what law they were applying that requirement, and they had not been able to answer; they had asked her how she came to speak the language so well, and she had replied that she was Hungarian, adding that there was no point trying to cheat the tourists.

She was right to the letter. Compulsory exchange for Western visitors to Hungary had been abolished in 1978: three years before that night. The guards were demanding something no rule provided for any longer, and to the direct question they could not answer, because there was nothing to cite.

It is a Kafka parable turned inside out. In "Before the Law" a man from the country waits his whole life in front of a doorkeeper who will not let him in, and learns at the end that the door was meant for him alone. At the Hungarian border there was a doorkeeper, and a door, and no law; and it was enough for someone to speak the doorkeeper's language and ask which law it was for the door to open. The rest of us, outside the car, were exactly in the condition van Gennep calls the margin: no longer on this side, not yet on that one, and without a word to understand what was happening.

That night, language was the passport behind the passport. It is a thread that will run through the next two Parts, because at a border language always decides something: whoever speaks the other side's language does not just get through, they know what to ask.

The door has said what it had to say here, and you can see it in what it needs in order to exist: a house, that is, someone who can say *this is my threshold*, and someone else who recognises it. When that thing grows large, it becomes a line on a map; and when the line is drawn by someone who has never been to the place, the story of Part Three begins.

## Part Three: The Line, the Border That Declares

### The furrow and the god

Before it was a line on paper, a border was a furrow in the earth and a stone set at a point.

The furrow is the gesture of foundation: you plough around what will become the city, and that cut in the soil separates inside from outside before there is anything to separate. The stone is the gesture of someone who stops. Zanini, in his book on the meanings of the border, observes that for peoples on the move, driving a stone or a post into the ground serves to found space afresh each time: only then does the land around it become habitable. A border, in that case, is not the end of a possession. It is the act of declaring that from now on this point has a name.

The Romans had a god for that stone, and his name was Terminus. Not a god of walls or of armies: a god of boundary markers, objects a farmer could shift in the night. That it took a deity to hold a stone in place says how fragile the thing he held in place was.

The god has outlasted many frontiers. Régis Debray opens his defence of borders with a study by Hans Holbein the Younger for a stained-glass window, from 1525, depicting Terminus himself. More than a thousand years after the Roman cult had ended, a Renaissance patron wanted the god of boundaries in his window.

Then there is a word worth looking at closely, because it tells a different story from the one we attach to it. *Limes*, from which "limit" comes, did not originally mean a barrier. It meant the path between two fields, the strip of earth you walked on. And the *limes* of the Roman Empire, before it was a wall, was a network of roads with garrisons: a device for moving quickly along a strip, not for sealing it. In Latin, the border begins as a road.

Italian still keeps, in three words, three different relations to the other side, and English has the same three, though it uses them more loosely. *Confine* comes from *cum-finis*, the end two parties share: drawing it takes two, and Italian says one "borders with" someone, *confinare con*. *Frontiera*, frontier, comes from *fronte*, the forehead, the front: it faces the unknown or the enemy, and it is a band that can advance, like the American frontier moving west. *Limite*, limit, is what you do not go beyond. Anyone who keeps this typology in mind will understand, on reaching Part Four, what a wall is: a border from which one of the two parties has vanished. A border is made by two and holds for both; a wall is decided by one side alone, and the other side undergoes it.

### Territory as a modern invention

A state that coincides with a surface is a recent idea, and it needed tools: maps drawn to scale, theodolites and triangulation to measure land, cadastral registers that recorded every parcel with its owner's name, and offices able to keep all of it up to date.

Charles Maier told this story in a book that starts in 1500. His argument is that for centuries power was exercised over people, jurisdictions and overlapping rights, and only at a certain point learned to exercise itself over a continuous area, bounded and measured. The moment the state becomes a patch of colour on a map with a sharp edge is also the moment the idea is born that every point on the planet must belong to one state and one only.

Put like that it remains a formula, and a formula cannot be seen. So here is what a power exercised over people and rights, rather than over an area, looked like in practice.

Take the India of Aurangzeb, a contemporary of Louis XIV. An official is not paid a salary: he is assigned the revenues of a village. But the same village is also squeezed by the village headman, by the tax farmers and by the zamindars, each holding a different title to take something. "The claims of ownership," Maier writes, "were often fragmented or parceled: village headman, tax farmers, zamindars, and others came to be known as holding dependencies." So much so that receiving the revenues of a village, rather than an income paid directly, could become a burden for the official who got them. The question "whose village is this?" has no single answer: it depends on what you want from it. The tax itself was not reckoned on peasants one by one but on the village as a block, a single sum fixed for the year. The state did not see people, and it did not see a surface either. It saw a source of revenue with a hierarchy of claimants on top.

This is no Asian oddity. The same happened in Europe, and went on happening after the lines had been drawn. Maier recalls Peter Sahlins's study of the Pyrenees after the peace of 1659: for a long time the French and the Spanish defined their rights in terms of jurisdiction over particular sectors of economic and legal life, even when those sectors lay beyond the new frontier. Only a century later did the two states' claims come to cover every aspect of control over their own side. The line came first, and only afterwards the idea that everything inside it belongs to whoever drew it. The two things that now seem one and the same are a hundred years apart.

Then there was the power that was not a state at all. The East India companies, the Dutch VOC and its English rival, were private firms to which the law of the state granted the right to raise capital, to use violence and to punish: Maier calls them para-states. A trading company with its own army and the power to condemn, signing treaties with local rulers across Asia. Who was in charge in those ports was not a geographical question.

And finally there was a space nobody could claim: the sea. No state, short of a universal empire, could assert exclusive rights over maritime traffic. Violating passage and trade in time of peace was called piracy, a scourge any power could fight in the name of all. An area with no owner but with a rule, which is the exact opposite of the way we now think of space.

Writing about Vauban's fortresses, Maier uses a phrase that ties this Part to the first:

> The border membrane, in a very practical as well as theoretical way, established the space of the state.

He is a historian of territory, writing about seventeenth-century bastions, and he picks the word biologists use for the cell. The coincidence is not a pun. It says that the border of a state, like the border of a cell, does not contain space: it produces it.

The tool of that production is the cadastre — the operation of measuring every field and writing it into a register. Maier quotes the Prussian finance minister Karl August von Struensee, who wrote in 1800:

> Whoever seeks to govern a territory without this guiding thread will be tapping in the dark.

That measuring land was an act of power, and not an office routine, shows in who resisted it. For the Savoy cadastre, published in the autumn of 1738, the surveyors in many cases had to rely on the owners' own declarations, because the nobility distrusted a state that measured. The Marquis de la Roche's estate agent wrote to his master a sentence that holds for this whole Part:

> We are attacked on all sides. God, what a century!

And the delegation of nobles protested at being suspected of disloyalty, declaring themselves ready to see their lands pass to the crown, "of so little value as they are", provided they were not accused of a want of zeal. Letting yourself be measured and remaining master in your own house had become two things that no longer went together.

The detail that concerns us closely is that the cadastres were not all alike. The Theresian cadastre, Maier writes, was based on new surveys only in the Tyrol; elsewhere it made do with the declarations of the nobles, punished by confiscation if false. The Tyrol, the land where my family would be born, was actually measured.

A measured territory can then be divided in a new way. The furrow ploughed around the city had to be redone every year and held only as long as it could be seen; a line drawn on a cadastral map does not need to be visible to hold, because what keeps it standing is no longer the ground but the register. From that moment on, a border can exist in an office.

### "Natural" borders

At this point something curious happens. The line, the most artificial product there is, starts looking for a natural foundation.

A river, a watershed, a mountain range: these are called "natural" borders, and they are always called that when it suits. Nature draws no borders; at most it offers obstacles, and an obstacle becomes a border only if someone decides to use it as one. A river, after all, does what Simmel's bridge did in Part Two: it joins and separates in the same gesture. People fish it together, cross it to marry and to sell. It becomes a frontier only on the day someone decides that another state begins on the far side, and from then on the same water that held two villages together holds them apart.

The case I know best is my own. In 1919 the border between Italy and Austria was moved to the Brenner, and the argument was precisely that the Alpine watershed is a natural frontier. James Crawford, who gives a chapter to this border, opens his book right there: "High up on the Alpine watershed between Austria and Italy a border melts and creeps downhill." The glaciers retreat, the watershed shifts by metres, and the two states have had to accept that their natural border walks.

The natural border has a bigger problem than glaciers, though: the people who live on it. By taking the frontier to the watershed, Italy annexed a German-speaking population. The answer was not to move the line but to move the words. On 23 March 1923, by royal decree, the Italian state officially adopted the catalogue of place names compiled by Ettore Tolomei, 16,735 of them, to replace the German ones.

The line on the map had declared who was in charge; the dictionary of names enforced it. It is the continuation of the border by other means, and it anticipates by a century the questions of language that will return at the end of this Part.

Proof of how real a line becomes, once drawn, came from a man dead for five thousand years. When a mummy was found in the ice, high up, in 1991, the first question was not who he was: it was which state he was lying in. It was measured, and he turned out to be on the Italian side by a little over ninety metres. Ötzi, who crossed those ridges when there was neither an Italy nor an Austria, is today a posthumous citizen of the province of Bolzano.

### The border that crosses you

On 10 September 1919, at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, Section VI of the peace treaty with Austria settles who becomes what. It runs to thirteen articles, from 70 to 82: the document that decided my parents' nationality.

Article 70 states that every person possessing *pertinenza* in a territory of the former monarchy obtains ipso facto, to the exclusion of Austrian nationality, the nationality of the state now exercising sovereignty over it. *Pertinenza* — in German *Heimatrecht* — was membership of a municipality, recorded person by person by the Habsburg administration: not residence, not language, not birth. The English text of the treaty, which is as authoritative as the French and the Italian, leaves the word in Italian in brackets — "rights of citizenship (pertinenza)" — because English has no word for it.

For Italy there was an extra rule. Article 71 lays down that Italian nationality is not acquired ipso facto by those who held *pertinenza* without having been born in the territory, or who had acquired it after 24 May 1915, the day Italy entered the war. Two things were needed together: to belong to a municipality and to have been born there.

Article 82 completes the picture: "the status of a married woman will be governed by that of her husband, and the status of children under 18 years of age by that of their parents." In 1916 my grandmother's Austrian passport gave, under occupation, her husband's job, and as her surname her husband's surname. Four years later, the treaty that abolished that Austria laid down that her nationality too depended on the same man. My mother, who was eight, became Italian because her parents did.

Nobody in that family had moved. That is what I mean when I say there are borders you cross and borders that cross you: the second kind asks nothing of anyone, and the next day you are something else.

The sequel to this story comes eighty years later, and concerns those who did move. Many people from the Trentino had emigrated before 1920, above all to Brazil and Argentina, and since they had not exercised the right to opt for Italian citizenship provided by the treaty, they had stayed Austrian for good, they and their children. In 2000 an Italian law, no. 379, recognised the citizenship of their descendants. To obtain it you had to prove that you belonged to the Italian language group, and among the evidence admitted were certificates from Italian-language schools and family letters written in Italian. By 2010 applications numbered nearly forty-four thousand.

The border of 1919 reached the great-grandchildren in Nova Trento, in Brazil, and to cross back over it you needed a letter from your great-grandmother.

### Lines within the line

Let me go back to 1915 for a moment, because war shows something that peace hides: how many lines there are within a single line.

When Italy entered the war in May 1915, the Trentino was Austrian frontier territory, and the authorities put into effect a plan prepared months before. They moved more than seventy thousand civilians to the interior of the empire: Bohemia, Moravia, Lower and Upper Austria, the Salzburg region. Some ended up in the "wooden towns", the hutted camps of Mitterndorf, Braunau, Wagna; others were spread around the villages.

The historian Francesco Frizzera, who has studied these refugees, explains the criteria, and they are the criteria of a fortress. From the strongholds of Trento and Riva, those who could not show they had enough supplies were sent away — the mouths to feed in case of siege. A woman with four small children fell exactly into that category. In the same plan one reads that the railwaymen of the southern lines were not evacuated: they were needed. That is why in my family the war runs like a line dividing the house: my grandmother and the four children near Salzburg, my grandfather still on the railway in the Trentino.

There is also a legal distinction that Marco Bellabarba and Gustavo Corni ask us not to lose: evacuation and internment are two different things, even if to those who endured them they might look alike. The evacuee was removed; the internee was suspected. Those Trentini judged unreliable for political reasons ended up at Katzenau, near Linz. And suspicion was not the command's private affair: Frizzera notes that behind many evacuations lay doubts, more or less well founded, about the political reliability of the people removed from the war zone.

The war zone itself was a line within the line, with a name and a bureaucracy: the *engeres Kriegsgebiet*, the Rayonskommando, the fortress court of Trento. Read the passport in the Prologue and this architecture shows through like a watermark: the state, the zone, the fortress, the checkpoints, and the name of the closed checkpoint struck out in pen.

Twenty-eight years later, the same thing happened again, to the same woman. In 1943 my grandmother was evacuated for the second time in her life, and this time the front was the sky. The first bombing of Trento came on 2 September 1943, some two hundred dead in a single raid; from then on the city averaged five air-raid alarms a day, and whoever could went up into the valleys. By 30 April 1945 the evacuees numbered nearly twenty-three thousand. Between November 1944 and April 1945, on the Brenner railway between Verona and Innsbruck, the Allies dropped ten thousand tons of bombs: the same route along which, twenty-eight years earlier, a stamp had recorded my grandmother's passage.

The second time, though, evacuation coincided with a return, and she chose the destination herself. There is a bitter symmetry in this story. In 1915 a state moved her far from home because her city had become a fortress; in 1943 she went back to her birthplace because her city had become a target.

But the lines within the line are not only military, and some are older than the states that contain them. Three historic linguistic minorities live in the Trentino today, recognised by its Statute of Autonomy: the Ladins of the Val di Fassa, the Mòcheni of the Fersina valley, the Cimbrians of Luserna. The Mòcheni valley, an island of German speech in Italian territory, is a few kilometres from where I live. No state frontier has ever coincided with that language border, and none has erased it.

Languages crossed borders too, and in the most prosaic way there is: by going to work. Between the late nineteenth century and the Great War tens of thousands of Trentini left for the Vorarlberg and the northern Tyrol to build railways, and from those building sites they brought home the name of their trade. Lionello Groff's *Dizionario trentino-italiano* records it as a word of the dialect:

> aisenponer = lavoratore nella costruzione di ferrovie (Ric.). (barb. Einsenbahner).
> *aisenponer = worker on railway construction (Ric.). (barb. Einsenbahner).*

The word came into the Trentino lexicon with a broader meaning than the German one, because *Eisenbahner*, in Germany and Austria, means a railway employee, whereas here it means the man who builds the railway, the labourer on the line. The abbreviation in brackets points to Groff's source, Giovanni Ricci's *Vocabolario trentino-italiano*, printed in Trento in 1904: the word was already recorded as Trentino before the Great War, while the emigrants were coming and going.

Two details in the way the dictionary treats it say more than an explanation would. The first is the abbreviation *barb.*, which in the table of abbreviations means "barbarism, that is, belonging to another language": the dictionary takes the word in and in the same breath marks it as foreign — lets something through and stamps it. The second is that the German source word is misspelt, *Einsenbahner*, with one *n* too many. Crossing a language border, words get bent, and even the person who gathers them into a dictionary keeps the bend.

My grandfather worked on the railway as a labourer, on the Valsugana line, and at home his job went by just that name, *aisenponer*. It was not a nickname for him; it was the name of the work. And that German word had stayed in a family where nobody spoke German and nobody had ever crossed the Brenner for work.

Languages, then, have borders of their own, unlike those of states: porous, crossed by working, leaving words behind as residue. Today the Statute guarantees those minorities, among other things, the right to set an official spelling. It is a border drawn where there was none before, because a spoken language has no orthography; and it is drawn to protect those inside, not to keep anyone out. It is proof, if proof were needed, that drawing a line is not in itself a hostile act.

That the relation decides, and not the form, is clearer still if you compare two cases of the same device: the enclave, a piece of one state surrounded by another.

At Baarle, on the border between the Netherlands and Belgium, the line snakes among the houses and runs through them. When it passes through a building, the two parts belong to two different states, and to decide where taxes are paid there is a local custom: you pay to the country where your front door is. The result was that for years residents moved their front doors a few feet, to end up in the cheaper country. Bonnett, who went there, says that "door shunting" has died out but its memory is kept alive, along with the markers on the ground: some borders are marked by white crosses painted on the pavement, but there are too many to signpost them all, and on one 160-metre stretch of Kapelstraat he walked in a straight line across five national borders in under a minute.

Here the argument becomes literal. It is not the line that decides where you are: it is the door. And if the door decides, you can move the door.

The chitmahals, nearly two hundred enclaves between India and Bangladesh, are the same device and its exact opposite. They ranged from almost twenty-six square kilometres for the largest to fifty-three square metres for one of the smallest, and they included counter-enclaves — enclaves inside enclaves — and even the world's only counter-counter-enclave. For decades the people born there lived inside a piece of a state they could not reach, without schools, hospitals or papers, because to get to their own country they would have had to cross the other one. It took until 2015 for India and Bangladesh to exchange them.

Two legends circulate about the origin of those lines. The first says they are lands won and lost at chess between a maharaja and a nawab in the early eighteenth century. The second, that in 1947 a drunk British cartographer knocked over his inkpot on exactly that stretch of map, and the blots, drying, became borders. The second is false and the first is nearly true, because those borders really do come from deals and favours between eighteenth-century princes. But that such stories get told says something precise: when a line looks absurd to the people living inside it, the popular explanation puts it down to a game or an accident — and in both cases to someone who was not looking.

There remains the case in which it does become hostile. When the line stops letting people through, and starts answering to only one of its two sides, it changes nature and changes name. It is called a wall, and it is Part Four.

## Part Four: The Wall, the Border That Denies

### More walls, less sovereignty

On the evening of 9 November 1989 the Berlin Wall came down, and for a few years it seemed that every wall had come down with it, as if that way of treating borders had gone out of use too. Then people started building new ones, and more of them than before.

Wendy Brown devoted a book to this paradox whose title already states its thesis: *Walled States, Waning Sovereignty*. Her idea is that today's walls are a sign not of strong states but of states that no longer control what they claim to control: capital that moves at a click, firms larger than governments, illegal traffic no customs post intercepts, religious allegiances that have never coincided with a map. The wall, then, is a theatrical answer to powerlessness. It serves to display a power that is no longer exercised anywhere else.

The thesis has a precedent four thousand years old, and it lies in the records of a king who did not notice. David Frye, who wrote a history of civilisation as seen from its walls, points to a coincidence in the lists by which the kings of Ur gave each year of their reign a name. Shulgi, around 2000 BC, had filled his years with victories; after his defeat of Anshan, in the thirty-fourth, the victories stop appearing. His thirty-seventh year bears the only title the king could give it, and it is not a battle:

> The Year the Wall of the Land Was Built.

The wall enters the annals at exactly the point where the conquests end. And one of his successors, Shu-Sin, had a barrier of mud bricks and trenches extended against the Syrian nomads, known — in Frye's words — by "the excruciatingly clumsy phrase 'That Which Keeps the Amorites at a Distance'". Neither work saved Ur, which fell anyway, and not to the Amorites. Clumsy or not, the name remains the most honest ever given to a border, because it declares in full the half the king cared about. Keeping out. The other half is not in the name.

The journalist Tim Marshall, after thirty years as a correspondent in the Balkans, Afghanistan and the Middle East, set out to count them. In 2018 he wrote that "at least sixty-five countries, more than a third of the world's nation states, have built barriers along their borders; half of those erected since the Second World War sprang up between 2000 and now". And he added that within a few years European nations could have more miles of walls, fences and barriers on their borders than there were at the height of the Cold War. Those are the figures of 2018, and they have not gone down since.

Wendy Brown's survey is instructive even as a list. Uzbekistan fenced out Kyrgyzstan in 1999 and Afghanistan in 2001, officially against "Islamic terrorists". In 2003 Botswana began an electrified fence along its border with Zimbabwe, with the pretext of stopping an outbreak of foot-and-mouth disease among cattle and the intention of stopping people too. The health pretext and the security pretext take turns, and the wall nearly always goes up where the neighbour is poorer.

Brown also notes the effect these devices have on those who get round them. At the border between the United States and Mexico, she writes, "mountain, desert, sea, and tunnel smuggling operations have grown more sophisticated in response to the barrier". The wall does not stop the crossing: it makes it dearer, more professional and more dangerous. The winners are those who sell the crossing.

There is one last reason these structures multiply, and it is the one least often admitted: they work superbly as images. A wall can be photographed, inaugurated, shown off in an election campaign. A border that works — that filters — cannot be seen.

The extreme case of this theatre is on the border between India and Pakistan, at Attari–Wagah, where every afternoon, before sunset, a ceremony is staged: India's Border Security Force and the Pakistan Rangers perform the Beating Retreat. A parade, iron gates swinging open, flags lowered in step, a handshake between soldiers and the gates closing again, while the crowds on both sides cheer and shout. It has gone on since 1959, at border pillar number 102, and the official Indian tourism site presents it as "a symbol of bilateral cooperation".

The phrase sounds ironic and is in fact exact, which is why this ceremony proves more than a hundred pages of theory. To stage hostility you have to rehearse it together. The flags must come down at the same speed, the steps must match, the gates must open and close in unison: if either side missed its cue, the spectacle of enmity would collapse. The most exhibited border in the world is also the most coordinated: the proof in uniform of what the Italian etymology says: the *cum-finis* is the end two parties share, and neither can hold it alone.

There is also a detail that concerns van Gennep. This is a rite of passage performed every day by people who do not pass: nobody crosses anything, the soldiers each go back to their own side, the crowd goes home. The rite is not for whoever crosses. It is for the border, which needs remaking every evening.

### In defence of borders

At this point it is worth handing the floor to someone who defends borders, and choosing an awkward one.

Debray's *Éloge des frontières*, the Tokyo lecture from which Part One took the insect's keratin, is built entirely on one distinction, and its most quoted formula is this:

> Le mur interdit le passage; la frontière le régule.
> *The wall forbids passage; the frontier regulates it.*

And he adds that to call a frontier a sieve is to do it justice:

> Dire d'une frontière qu'elle est une passoire, c'est lui rendre son dû : elle est là pour filtrer.
> *To say of a frontier that it is a sieve is to give it its due: it is there to filter.*

Whoever confuses the two, Debray argues, ends up demanding the abolition of borders and finds walls instead: because where no line is recognised, the only defence left is the barrier.

It is the same figure this book started from, in a political version: the filter, not the barrier.

The argument is strong, but it has a blind spot that needs pointing out. In the whole of his eulogy Debray never mentions a single person who died at a border. He speaks of symbols, identities, belonging, and is silent about what happens to those who try to pass where passing is impossible. A filtering frontier is a reasonable thing as long as you look at it from the side of the people who administer it. From the side of the people being filtered it is the device that decides whether you arrive or drown, and that decision has no appeal.

Sandro Mezzadra and Brett Neilson, who come from a political position very far from Debray's, reach an observation that resembles his. They say they have grown uncomfortable with "the fixation in many critical border studies as well as activist circles on the image of the wall", because taking the wall as the icon of contemporary borders "can paradoxically reinforce the spectacle of the border" and loses sight of the fact that today's borders above all filter and select. They do not separate inside from outside: they sort people into categories, and give each category different rights.

Between Debray and them lies the argument of this book. The filter is what makes a border a border, and it is also the place where it is decided who is worth what.

What sorting means in practice can be seen in the device that replaced the old quotas: the points system. It was born in the 1970s in Australia and Canada, which moved from migration policies based on race to policies based on a score, and from there it spread to countries as different as the United Kingdom, Germany, Singapore, the Czech Republic and the Netherlands.

It works like this. Whoever wants to come in declares their qualities, and each is worth a number of points: education, professional skills, knowledge of the language, family ties, health, age, religion, the money they hold, and in some countries familiarity with the national culture and values, measured by a citizenship test. Whoever clears a threshold gets in. Whoever has more points gets more: fast tracks to permanent residence, sometimes to citizenship.

But the decisive detail lies elsewhere, and it is what makes this border different from all those before it. The scale is not fixed. It moves with the needs of the labour market and with the number of applicants who share the same characteristics. A person's score, that is, does not depend on them alone: it depends on how many others resemble them at that moment. The same degree, the same trade, the same age are worth more or less depending on the year. There is no threshold to reach once and for all. There is a price.

And after entry the sorting goes on. Many skilled workers get only temporary access, "subject to visa conditions that, if violated, turn them into deportable subjects": they are inside, working and paying taxes, one step from being sent back. The border, in this way, no longer separates two categories, citizen and foreigner. It produces a continuous scale, full of grey areas, in which the same person can stand in several positions at once.

The passport in the Prologue described my grandmother by the colour of her hair and her husband's job. The points system describes whoever arrives with a number, and that number changes by itself while they wait.

The most effective border built in this century, which appears on no map, shows that the filter counts for more than the barrier. It is the system by which China controls what information enters and leaves its network, and which in English is called the Great Firewall — a word borrowed from fire safety, where a firewall stops flames passing from one building to the next, and applied to a border with no space. Marshall, who gives it the first chapter of his book, catches its contradiction: the same country wants to stop the circulation of ideas while building an economy that lives on data exchange with the rest of the world. His formula is that the authorities "must divide in order to unite".

That border does not block, it selects, and that is why it works. A wall closing the network would cost the Chinese economy more than it would earn the Chinese government; a filter that lets trade through and stops dissent costs much less. It is the membrane of Part One put at the service of a state: it chooses what comes in, and the one choosing is not the one receiving.

And there is one last symmetry. In Part One, Winograd and Flores argued that information does not cross the boundaries of a system on its own, because meaning is built by the receiver. The firewall is the political admission of that thesis: if the receiver makes the sense, then governing a country takes more than controlling what is said. You have to control what arrives.

This is where the objection left open in Part One can be taken up. Stafford Beer told Maturana and Varela that if a living thing is defined by its capacity to produce its own components and its own edge, then a firm, a family and a state are alive in the strict sense, because that is exactly what they do. The two did not follow him, and there is a reason not to: a cell does not choose its edge, while a state decides where to put it, how far to open it and for whom. But the objection holds on one point, and it is the point of this Part. An institution that closes itself behaves like the immune system Tauber described when it stops working: it forgets that its outline is crossed all the time by things useful to it, and treats as foreign everything that touches it. The difference is that when an organism does this, we call it disease. A state calls it security.

### The sea has no gates

Of all borders, the maritime one is the most instructive, because it has no gates to list.

Kant, in 1795, had already put the sea into his page on hospitality: the uninhabitable parts of the earth's surface, ocean and desert, split the human community, but "ships and camels" make it possible to come into touch across those unappropriated regions. And he condemned the inhospitality of the inhabitants of sea coasts who go "plundering ships in neighbouring seas or making slaves of shipwrecked mariners".

Two hundred and thirty years later the Mediterranean is the deadliest frontier in the world, and what makes it so is not the water.

One example. In 1979 a group of West German citizens, among them the writer Heinrich Böll, chartered a cargo ship to rescue the Vietnamese boat people — those who, after the end of the war, left Vietnam in overloaded boats unfit for the sea. In June 1979, the worst month, more than fifty-four thousand of them reached neighbouring countries and Hong Kong; over twenty years there were eight hundred thousand, and according to the UN High Commissioner for Refugees between two hundred thousand and two hundred and fifty thousand died at sea, drowned or killed by pirates. The ship was called the Cap Anamur, and the name passed to the organisation. Twenty-five years later, in the summer of 2004, a ship of that name lay for days off Sicily with thirty-seven shipwrecked people on board, while governments argued over whose job it was to open a port. The ship that had been born to pick up the shipwrecked found itself the problem.

Another. In 2018 the Asso 28, an Italian-flagged tug serving an oil platform off Libya, rescued a boat and took the hundred and one people it had saved back to Libya, after taking on board a man identified as a member of the Libyan coastguard. The act has a name in international law, *refoulement*, and Kant had already ruled it out in a clause of a few words: a stranger may be sent away "if this can be done without causing his death".

Here the argument of this book shows its hard face. A filtering border is necessary; but when the filter is pushed offshore and handed to another country's coastguard, as happened with Libya's, the border is no longer a line two parties recognise. It is a function a state exports, so as not to have to look at it.

### Suspicion on both sides

There is one category of people that borders produce reliably: those in between.

In 1915, in the Austrian Trentino, Italian-speaking civilians were suspect in Austria's eyes. German-speaking deputies denounced a military authority that saw "a spy everywhere and behind every Italian Tyrolean". The army removed thousands, and interned those it judged politically unreliable at Katzenau.

On the other side the symmetrical thing happened. When the Italian army occupied the southern valleys, the Trentini living there were treated not as liberated brothers but as suspects. General Antonio Cantore, commander of the Fifth Army Corps, said it without mincing words: "You Trentini are all Austria-lovers [...] My soldiers are convinced they are fighting a war of liberation, not of occupation." Some thirty-five thousand people were moved to other provinces of the Kingdom of Italy, and some interned on suspicion of Austrian sympathies.

Francesco Frizzera named this condition with a phrase that says everything: *cittadini dimezzati*, citizens cut in half. Too Austrian for Italy, too Italian for Austria. It is not an individual misfortune; it is a property of the border. Whoever lives on it is the living proof that the categories do not hold, and the categories defend themselves by taking away half their rights.

The category that cuts people in half can also be invented out of nothing, and then the mechanism is visible to the naked eye.

After the armistice of 8 September 1943, the Italian soldiers captured by the Germans were not classed as prisoners of war. From 20 September, by Hitler's decision, they were called *Italian military internees*. The name described nothing new. It served to remove them from the protection of the Geneva Convention and from the oversight of the Red Cross, and to make it possible to put them to forced labour. Among them was my father, Lino, called up and deported to a camp in the Sudetenland, where he stayed nearly two years.

Of that time I know almost nothing, because he did not like to talk about it, and that silence is part of the story as much as the facts. He passed on one memory only, and it is of the end. Freed, he was put on a train without knowing where it was going. He understood he was heading home to Italy only when he saw the name of a station, Garmisch-Partenkirchen, which he knew from the Winter Olympics of 1936. From there the line runs down towards Innsbruck and the Brenner — to the same pass where, twenty-nine years earlier, his mother-in-law had got a stamp in her passport.

Two years of captivity reached the family as the name of a station.

In peacetime the same logic works through words. Nadan Petrovic, tracing the history of the right of asylum in Italy from the Constitution of 1948 to the Geneva Convention of 1951, ratified by Italy in 1954, and on to the European directives, shows that behind the terms lie destinies: refugee, displaced person, evacuee, asylum seeker, economic migrant. Each word opens or closes a right, and the choice of word comes before the judgement of the case. It is the same operation this book has carried out with border, frontier, limit, wall — except that here the vocabulary decides who gets in.

### Trespassing

Italian has a verb that does two jobs at once: *sconfinare*. It means to cross the border of a field or a state, and it means to overstep, to go beyond what is due, to stray off the subject. English needs two words for it, *to trespass* and *to digress*. It is the same double sense as *errare*, to wander and to err, which gives this series its name.

In 1978 I went from Vienna to Prague. To enter Czechoslovakia you needed a passport and a visa, and for the visa you had to go to the embassy, where they handed each of us five green carbon-copy slips, to be filled in by pen. At the border they let in one car at a time. They had us get out at a hut where five soldiers stood in a row in front of us, and each took one of the five slips. They noted the car's number plate and the serial numbers of our cameras, because those objects had to leave the country as they had come in. Then they let us through. I have always imagined that after we passed, those slips ended up in a vast room, blown in with a hair dryer.

That was not a wall: it was a pedantic membrane. It filtered, recorded, counted, and in exchange for five carbon copies it let you through. The regime that had built the Iron Curtain treated the Western tourist as a resource: tied to that entry was also the obligation to change a minimum sum of currency, which in Czechoslovakia in 1977 was ten dollars a day for ordinary tourists.

Three years later, in 1981, I made a long tour of Western Europe: Austria, Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, France, Switzerland. The frontiers were almost non-existent, but at every crossing the currency changed. Money made the border. And in our pockets we had traveller's cheques in US dollars — the one currency that belonged to none of the countries we were crossing and that each of them converted into its own. Traveller's cheques, come to think of it, were a small border rite too: you signed once when you bought them and a second time in front of whoever cashed them, passport in hand, so that the money crossed together with the identity of the person carrying it.

In 2002 that border disappeared too, replaced by a single currency. Meanwhile, elsewhere, new ones were being built.

That is why this Part does not end by condemning borders. It ends with a distinction, which is the argument of the book in its shortest form: it is not the border that kills, it is its closure. A line you can cross by paying a reasonable toll, showing a document, changing ten dollars and losing half a day is a nuisance. A line that cannot be crossed by any legal means forces whoever must cross it into the hands of those who sell the crossing, and makes them risk their lives. Between the two there is no wall. There is a gate someone has decided not to leave.

## Epilogue: The Limit of Thought

One border remains that we have not discussed, and it is the only one that cannot be crossed: the border of thought.

Kant takes it up in the *Prolegomena*, and separates two German words, *Grenze* and *Schranke*. *Grenze* is the border of a space: it presupposes that there is something on the other side, and so it is something positive. *Schranke* is pure negation, the barrier beyond which there is nothing to find. In Paul Carus's English the two become *bounds* and *limits*, and the sentence reads:

> For in all bounds there is something positive (e.g., a surface is the boundary of corporeal space, and is therefore itself a space, a line is a space, which is the boundary of the surface, a point the boundary of the line, but yet always a place in space), whereas limits contain mere negations.

The example is geometric and can be grasped at a glance. A border is made of the same stuff as what it separates, and belongs to both sides instead of lying between them like a void.

The English reader is lucky here, and it is worth saying why. Italian translations render *Grenze* as *limite* and *Schranke* as *confine*, so that the standard Italian edition says that in all *limits* there is something positive while *borders* contain mere negations — exactly the reverse of the scheme of this book, where the border is the positive thing with two sides and the limit is that beyond which there is nothing. Carus keeps the words the right way round: *bounds*, which have an outside, and *limits*, which do not. I am not correcting anyone's translators, who follow their own traditions. I am pointing out that the words with which we think about borders have borders of their own, and that these do not coincide from one language to another. When this book says *border*, it means Kant's *Grenze*.

Wittgenstein, in the preface to the *Tractatus*, says the same thing as a paradox: "in order to be able to draw a limit to thought, we should have to find both sides of the limit thinkable (i.e. we should have to be able to think what cannot be thought)". That is why the limit of thought is not a border line: there is nobody on the other side to share it with.

The brain, though, really does have an edge, and it is not metaphorical. Andrea Moro, in *The Boundaries of Babel*, has shown that human languages are not infinite in their forms, and the remarkable thing is not the thesis but the way it was made visible.

The grammars of the world, however different, share one property: their rules work on the structure of the sentence, not on words counted one by one. No human language has a rule of the type "negation always goes in fourth position". Such a rule is perfectly thinkable; it can be stated and it can be applied. It simply exists in no language. Real rules refer to chunks of sentence, to hierarchies, to relations between parts, never to a count. Linguists call it structure dependence, and the question that matters is whether it is a historical habit of the languages we know or a limit in the people who speak them.

The first answer came indirectly. Neil Smith and Ianthi Tsimpli worked with Christopher, a man born in England in 1962 who was diagnosed with brain damage at six weeks old. He learned to speak and walk late, and as an adult could not look after himself; but he read and translated some fifteen languages besides his own, and could pick up a new one by ear. In him the language faculty was intact while the rest was not, and that is what made him the decisive subject: faced with a new rule, he could not fall back on the general intelligence with which anyone else tackles a puzzle. They taught him a language invented for the purpose, Epun, in which some rules were impossible in the sense just described. "No matter how hard he tried," Moro writes, "Christopher could not learn the impossible rules", while the control group managed. The explanation is more interesting than the result: ordinary people could get by with general intelligence, solving the thing as a puzzle, while he, faced with those rules, had nothing to work with.

The second answer is direct, and turned the matter into a fact. Moro and his colleagues had volunteers learn two sets of rules of a language they did not know, some possible and some impossible, while measuring their brain activity. The result has two halves, and they must be held together, because taken separately they say opposite things. The first is that the subjects learned both kinds, and with the same accuracy: looking only at their answers, the border could not be seen at all. The second is that the brain treated them in opposite ways. As the subjects got better, activity in Broca's area, the region that deals with syntax, rose for the possible rules and fell for the impossible ones. The experiment, repeated with German speakers learning Japanese, gave the same result, which rules out any effect of kinship between the languages.

The brain, Moro writes, "has 'sorted out' the syntactic data, without the subjects' realizing it", sending only the sentences built on structure to the area predisposed for syntax. The others are learned all the same, but they are learned somewhere else, the way one learns a times table.

And this is the purest form of the border this book has been chasing. Not a barrier that prevents, because there is no prohibition, and the proof is that those rules are learned perfectly well. It is a membrane that chooses which way to send what. The freedom of language, which seems boundless, is fenced by a structure we did not decide on. It is the membrane of Part One turning up where it is least expected: thought, too, has an outline it built for itself.

George Spencer-Brown, in *Laws of Form*, builds a whole logical calculus starting not from a definition but from an order:

> Draw a distinction. Call it the first distinction. Call the space in which it is drawn the space severed or cloven by the distinction.

Before that order, of the two axioms with which the book opens, one concerns this book closely. Spencer-Brown calls it the law of crossing: "The value of a crossing made again is not the value of the crossing." Someone who crosses a line and then crosses it back has not made two journeys: they have returned, and the two gestures taken together are worth neither. A border, to be anything at all, needs someone to stay on the other side.

It is worth saying what this calculus is, because its claim is larger than it seems and it is not a metaphor. From the distinction alone, and the two laws that govern it, Spencer-Brown builds a system of signs one can calculate with: a single sign, the gesture of separating, and the rules that say what happens when it is repeated and when it is crossed twice. From this he derives what he calls the primary algebra, and shows that the postulates of Boolean logic — the rules by which true and false, "and" and "or", behave — can be proved as theorems of that system instead of having to be assumed. Logic does not lie beneath the distinction. It stands on top of it.

What matters for this book is the order of things. We usually think that objects exist first and then someone distinguishes them. Here it is the other way round: first there is the cut, and objects are what the cut leaves on either side. You do not distinguish between two things that were already there; you make two things by distinguishing. It is what a membrane does when it closes round a drop of liquid, and what a treaty does when it names a line along a mountain ridge where before there was only snow.

From that first cut, Spencer-Brown maintained, everything else can be rebuilt. It is perhaps the most economical way of putting what this book has tried to say: there is no knowledge without a cut, no identity without an edge, no meeting without two.

Zen, usually invoked to dissolve distinctions, has left the most exact image on this point. The most famous collection of koans is called *The Gateless Gate*: not the absence of a threshold, but a threshold without a leaf. You go through it because it is there.

Alastair Bonnett, who has written about borders as a geographer and admits that they "frighten and unnerve" him, puts the question in its simplest form: "where, in a borderless world, could we escape to? Where would it be worth going?" It is what this book's subtitle says, turned the way a traveller would turn it.

From Frank Jacobs, who wrote a column of cartographic curiosities for the *New York Times*, Bonnett also borrows an awkward expression: "Phantom Border Sadness", which Jacobs defines as "a slight pang of grief caused by the conviction that a world with one less border is also a bit less special". Bonnett himself calls it, "in an era in which we are constantly urged to pull down the barriers that separate us", a dangerous thought. It really is dangerous, and must be handled with care: nobody misses the checkpoint where they were searched, and nostalgia for frontiers is the raw material of every politics that promises to raise them again. But under the phrase there is something true, which is why I quote it: the variety of the world is made of thresholds too, and an entirely uniform planet would not be a freer planet. It would be a planet without places.

So the book's question is not whether the world would be better off without borders. It would not: it would not be anything at all, because nothing would remain distinct from anything else, and there would be no beyond left to reach. The question is which borders we choose, who draws them, and whether we keep them able to open.

In 1916 my grandmother needed sixteen pages of official paper to enter the city where her children were born. In 1920 my family changed state without moving. In 1978 I handed five green slips to five soldiers to see Prague. In 1981 money made the border. Today you pass the Brenner without stopping, and in 2016 there was talk of stopping people there again.

None of these borders was natural. All of them did something. And the difference between the ones we crossed and the ones that crossed us lies not in the line: it lies in who, that day, held the key.

## About the Author

Giovanni Martini is a sociologist living in Trentino, on the Pine' plateau in northern Italy. An omnivorous reader with a preference for speculative fiction, philosophy and science, he has always looked for connections between distant disciplines: physics and consciousness, East and West, myth and technology. He prefers unresolved tensions to easy syntheses. *Errancies* is the English counterpart of his Italian series *Collana Erranze*, and the place where he collects these border crossings.
